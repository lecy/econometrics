Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Multiverse Analysis

A note for Unit 23. Every empirical result rests on dozens of design decisions: which cases to keep, which controls to include, how to measure the outcome. A multiverse analysis runs the study under every defensible combination of those decisions and reports what happens to the result. It shows which decisions matter and which don’t. Applied across a literature, it shows a field which modeling choices it needs to settle.

Section one

Key concepts

The argument in five claims.

Key concepts

A published estimate is one path through a garden of choices.

Each choice was defensible, but so were the alternatives. Readers usually see only the path the authors took.

The multiverse is every path at once.

list the decisions → list the defensible options for each → run every combination

Forks are the decisions you vary on purpose.

Everything else is held fixed, as context or as standard controls, so that differences across models can be traced to the forks.

Impact is measured on one scale.

Express the estimate in effect-size units (here, standard deviations of the outcome), then ask how much it moves when a single fork changes.

The goal is a better default, not a better p-value.

Decisions with no impact can be settled by convention. Decisions with large impact need a strong argument, and a field that makes those arguments can agree on a robust modeling framework.

Section two

The idea: from one model to all of them

Why a single specification is not enough.

The Forking Paths guide describes the problem. Every study involves choices that are rarely fully specified in advance: how to treat outliers, which covariates to adjust for, how to measure the outcome, which subsample to use. Gelman and Loken called this a garden of forking paths. A researcher can walk through it honestly, one reasonable step at a time, and still end up at a result that depends on which path was taken.

A multiverse analysis (Steegen et al., 2016) turns the garden into the object of study. Instead of reporting one specification as “the” result, the analyst lists the decisions that could reasonably have gone another way, runs the model under every combination, and reports the whole distribution of estimates. Specification curve analysis (Simonsohn, Simmons, & Nelson, 2020) and vibration of effects (Patel, Burford, & Ioannidis, 2015) are close relatives that use the same logic.

The question changes from “Is the effect significant?” to two more useful ones:

  • How robust is the result? Does the estimate keep its sign and rough size across defensible specifications, or does it appear only on a few paths?
  • Which decisions drive it? Of all the choices an analyst makes, which ones actually move the estimate?

Section three

The framework

Enumerate the choices, multiply them out, and sort every variable into a role.

Step 1: Enumerate the decisions

Start from a baseline model, usually the specification reported in the paper. Then list the points where a defensible alternative exists. Decisions come in two broad kinds:

  • Data preparation: outlier rules, exclusion criteria, how missing values are handled, how a variable is coded (raw counts or per capita, continuous or categorical), which waves or years are included.
  • Model specification: which controls are included, the functional form of the outcome (levels or logs), non-linear terms, interaction terms, the estimator itself.

Step 2: Multiply them out

Every combination of one option per decision is one specification, one version of the study. The set of all of them is the multiverse. Its size is the product of the number of options at each decision:

3 decisions × 2 options each = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 specifications  ·  6 decisions with 2 options = 26 = 64

The multiverse grows fast. Ten binary decisions produce 1,024 models, which is why it pays to be deliberate about which decisions to include.

Step 3: Assign every choice a role

RoleWhat it meansExamples
ForksThe decisions you want to examine. Each one is varied, and the multiverse includes every combination.Include prior earnings or not; trim outliers or not; logged or raw outcome.
ContextChoices held fixed in every model, either because they define the question or because they are standard practice. They are not tested, but they are reported.The sample frame, the estimator, robust standard errors.
ControlsCovariates included in every specification. They are part of the baseline, not under examination.Age, education, and other standard demographic controls.

The line between forks and the rest is a research choice. A control becomes a fork once you have a reason to ask whether it matters. Keeping the number of forks small makes the results readable. Holding the context fixed means that any difference between two models can be traced to the forks that differ between them.

One practical rule: hold the sample constant across specifications whenever possible. If adding a control drops rows because of missing values, the estimate changes for two reasons at once, and you can’t tell which one did the work.

Section four

Measuring the impact of a decision

Put every estimate on one scale, then compare models that differ in a single fork.

A common scale: effect-size units

Raw coefficients are hard to compare across specifications, especially when a fork changes how the outcome is measured. Converting each estimate into effect-size units solves this. Here we use the standardized mean difference: the treatment coefficient divided by the standard deviation of the outcome.

d = coefficient on treatment ÷ SD of the outcome  ·  d = 0.10 means one-tenth of a standard deviation

These are the same units used in meta-analysis (Unit 24), so a multiverse estimate can be compared directly with published effect sizes.

Leverage: how much does one decision move the estimate?

For each fork, compare every pair of specifications that are identical except for that fork. The average change in d when the fork is switched is its leverage. An equivalent shortcut is to regress the estimates from all specifications on indicator variables for the forks. Each coefficient is that fork’s leverage, holding the other forks fixed.

Report two more things alongside leverage:

  • Significance by fork: the share of specifications with p < .05 under each option. A fork can move the estimate a little and still decide whether it crosses the threshold.
  • Sign flips: whether any specifications change the direction of the effect, and which forks they share.

A caution about p-values in the leverage regression. All specifications use the same data, so they are not independent observations. Treat the regression as a way to rank how much each decision matters, not as a hypothesis test.

Section five

Worked example: does job training raise earnings?

Eight specifications, three forks: one that matters for theoretical reasons, one that matters because of five people, and one that doesn’t matter at all.

The data are the classic LaLonde (1986) job training sample as distributed in R’s MatchIt package: 185 participants in the National Supported Work program and 429 non-participants from a national survey, 614 people in all. The outcome is 1978 earnings (re78, SD = $7,471). Because the comparison group was not randomized, estimates depend heavily on modeling choices. That is exactly the setting where a multiverse is informative. Randomized versions of the same program put the benefit at roughly $1,800 (Dehejia & Wahba, 1999).

  • Controls (every model): age, education, race, marital status.
  • Context (every model): OLS, heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors.
  • Fork A, prior earnings: exclude or include 1974 and 1975 earnings.
  • Fork B, trim outliers: keep everyone, or drop the top 1% of 1978 earnings (above $25,565).
  • Fork C, degree indicator: exclude or include an indicator for no high school degree.

Reading the results

A decision with no impact (Fork C). Adding the degree indicator moves d by 0.000 on average, and no more than 0.004 in any pair of specifications. Education is already in the model, so the indicator adds little new information. This is useful to know: a reader who objects to the indicator, or insists on it, can be told the choice doesn’t affect the conclusion. Forks like this can be settled by convention.

A decision that matters for a substantive reason (Fork A). Including 1974 and 1975 earnings raises the estimate by 0.054 SD and, in the untrimmed sample, is the difference between a significant and a non-significant result. That makes sense: the program enrolled people with weak earnings histories, so without prior earnings the comparison group looks better off for reasons that have nothing to do with training. Theory supports including them, and the multiverse shows how much that argument matters.

A decision that matters for a fragile reason (Fork B). Dropping the top 1% of earners removes just five people, and all five were program participants. Losing them cuts the estimate roughly in half and eliminates significance in every specification. The positive finding leans heavily on a handful of very successful participants. That isn’t necessarily wrong, since some programs do produce a few large successes, but it is exactly the kind of fact a single reported specification would hide. The right response is an argument about whether these cases are errors, legitimate outliers, or the point of the program, not a quiet choice of whichever rule gives the better result.

The code

library(sandwich); library(lmtest)
data("lalonde", package = "MatchIt")
d    <- lalonde
sd_y <- sd(d$re78)
cut  <- quantile(d$re78, 0.99)

# the multiverse: every combination of three binary forks
grid <- expand.grid(prior = c(FALSE, TRUE),
                    trim  = c(FALSE, TRUE),
                    nodeg = c(FALSE, TRUE))

res <- do.call(rbind, lapply(seq_len(nrow(grid)), function(i) {
  g  <- grid[i, ]
  dd <- if (g$trim) d[d$re78 <= cut, ] else d
  f  <- "re78 ~ treat + age + educ + race + married"       # controls
  if (g$prior) f <- paste(f, "+ re74 + re75")               # fork A
  if (g$nodeg) f <- paste(f, "+ nodegree")                  # fork C
  m  <- lm(as.formula(f), data = dd)
  ct <- coeftest(m, vcov = vcovHC(m, "HC1"))["treat", ]     # context
  data.frame(g, n = nrow(dd), b = ct[1], se = ct[2],
             p = ct[4], d = ct[1] / sd_y)
}))

# leverage of each fork, holding the others fixed
coef(lm(d ~ prior + trim + nodeg, data = res))

Section six

From one study to a field: proposing robust frameworks

The payoff is cumulative. A multiverse tells the next researcher where the real arguments are.

A multiverse analysis of one paper answers a narrow question: how fragile is this result? The larger goal is to advance a research domain by turning scattered, undisclosed choices into a shared, defensible way of modeling the problem.

  1. Map the decisions. Identify which forks are high-leverage and which are not. Low-leverage decisions can be standardized by convention, and arguments about them can stop.
  2. Argue the high-leverage forks on the merits. Each consequential decision needs a justification from theory, the design, or the data, as with prior earnings above. The multiverse doesn’t choose the answer, but it shows where an answer is needed.
  3. Propose a reference specification. Combine the best-justified option at each high-leverage fork into a recommended modeling framework for the field, and report the full multiverse around it.
  4. Make it the default. Later studies preregister the reference specification, report the multiverse as a robustness appendix, and explain any departures. Results become comparable across studies, and meta-analyses (Unit 24) stop mixing estimates from incompatible models.

This is how the best-known multiverse studies have changed their fields. They did not just show that results were fragile. They showed which choices were responsible, and they changed what a credible analysis in that literature looks like.

Section seven

Multiverse studies to read

Applications rather than methods papers: each takes a live research question and runs the multiverse on it.

  • Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman, & Vanpaemel (2016). The paper that named the method, built around a reanalysis of a published study claiming that women’s religiosity and political attitudes shift with their fertility cycle. Across the defensible ways of processing the data, most combinations produced large p-values, so the finding was too sensitive to processing choices to support a firm conclusion.
  • Orben & Przybylski (2019). Does digital technology use harm adolescent well-being? Specification curves across three large datasets (combined N > 355,000) found a negative but small association, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. The paper moved a heated public debate toward a question of magnitude.
  • Rohrer, Egloff, & Schmukle (2017). Does birth order shape personality? Thousands of specifications confirmed a small effect on intellect and found no meaningful effects on narrower traits such as risk taking, patience, trust, or political orientation.
  • Patel, Burford, & Ioannidis (2015). An epidemiology version, “vibration of effects”: associations between exposures and mortality were re-estimated under every combination of adjustment variables, showing that some associations change size and even direction depending on which covariates are included.

For how far analytic choices can spread results when whole teams make them independently, see the many-analyst studies in Unit 22, especially Breznau et al. (2022).

Section eight

Getting started: an AI prompt for a multiverse replication

Once you have a dataset and a baseline model, an AI coding agent can build and run the multiverse for you.

The Multiverse Analysis AI Prompt Guide is a fill-in-the-blanks prompt for Claude Code, ChatGPT Codex, or a similar agent. You supply the paper, the baseline model, the data file, and the data dictionary, then name three design choices and three control variables as forks. The prompt asks the agent to:

  1. reproduce the paper’s baseline estimate, and stop if it can’t;
  2. build every combination of the forks (64 specifications with six binary forks) on a constant sample;
  3. apply standard defaults that are not forks: robust standard errors in every model, plus robustness runs with the top and bottom 1% trimmed and with the data split into bottom, middle, and top thirds;
  4. report a specification curve, a leverage table in effect-size units, the effect of each fork on significance, and any sign flips;
  5. leave the judgment about which choices are most justifiable to you.

Treat the agent’s output as a first draft. Check the baseline reproduction, read the code, and write the interpretation yourself.
