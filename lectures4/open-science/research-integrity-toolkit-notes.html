Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

A Research Integrity Toolkit

A reading guide for the 77-slide deck research_integrity_slides, the framework table it is built from, and the 27-technique toolkit. The organizing idea: sort each problem by who produces it — an individual, a team, a colluding group, or a whole field — because that decides which tools can see it and which remedies can fix it.

Section one

Key concepts

The ideas the deck and the framework table depend on, each stated as a claim.

Key concepts

Research integrity problems are sorted by unit of analysis, not by how bad they are.

  • Tier 1 — individual: data fabrication, image manipulation, plagiarism and text recycling, research methods errors
  • Tier 2a — team: collaborative pipeline failure, p-hacking and selective reporting, HARKing, fragile findings (the garden of forking paths), publication bias as a team decision
  • Tier 2b — group / collusion: citation rings and uncritical peer review, fake peer review rings, paper mills and predatory journals, institutional cover-up
  • Tier 3 — field / community: field-level publication bias, incentive structures, lack of replication culture, poor data stewardship, reviewer burnout

The question each tier asks is “where does the decision that causes the problem get made?” — one person’s keyboard, a lab’s shared pipeline, a network of authors and editors, or the reward system of an entire discipline.

Every problem row runs the same four steps: Intro → Evidence of scope → Detection → Prevention.

What is it? → How big is it? → How would we catch it? → How do we stop it happening?

The deck gives each step one slide, so every problem gets four slides. Learning the rhythm makes 77 slides feel like 18 short, parallel stories.

Integrity is broader than fraud.

The framework deliberately puts honest failures — coding bugs, broken team pipelines, technically defensible but fragile specifications — alongside fabrication. A spreadsheet error that reverses a policy conclusion damages the evidence base as surely as a made-up dataset does.

Most detection tools detect inconsistency, not fraud.

  • GRIM proves a reported mean is impossible; it cannot say whether the cause is a typo or fabrication.
  • statcheck finds p-values that do not match their test statistics.
  • A bibliometric flag is a starting point for inquiry, not a finding of misconduct.
  • A multiverse makes fragility visible without accusing anyone of anything.

Moving from “this is inconsistent” to “this is misconduct” takes process evidence — raw data, lab records, registrations — and usually a formal investigation.

The tools that work at one level do not work at another.

A paper-mill manuscript can pass every single-paper check; its signature only appears when you cluster thousands of papers. Publication bias leaves no trace in any one study; it only shows up in the distribution of many. HARKing has no automated detector at all. Choosing the tool starts with naming the level of the problem.

Three of these problems are econometrics problems wearing a different label.

  • Forking paths = the specification and control-variable choices you make in every regression
  • Publication bias = a selection problem that inflates effect sizes, and the reason meta-analysis needs bias corrections like PET-PEESE
  • Computational reproducibility = whether your own R Markdown file runs from raw data to final table on someone else’s machine

Section two

The framework: problems by unit of analysis

Why the tiers exist, what each tier’s tools can see, and the whole framework on one table.

Why the unit of analysis matters

The framework could have sorted problems by severity (fraud first, sloppiness last) or by discipline. It sorts them instead by the level at which the problem is produced, and the payoff is practical: the level determines what evidence a problem leaves behind, which in turn determines what kind of tool can detect it and who has the power to prevent it.

TierWhat the tools examineWho can prevent it
1 — IndividualA single paper’s statistics, images, or text. Most tools need no raw data.Journals at submission, data-sharing mandates, audit trails, whistleblower protection

Read down the middle column and the input changes character at each step: one document, then many results, then a network, then a system. Read down the right-hand column and the remedy moves from sanctioning a person to redesigning incentives. A tool built for one row is blind in the others — GRIM cannot see a citation ring, and network analysis cannot see an impossible mean.

Tier 1: individual misconduct and error

These are the problems one person can produce alone: inventing data, doctoring a figure, copying text, or making a coding mistake. Their evidence is sitting in the published paper, which is why Tier 1 tools are the most mature and the most automated. The headline numbers: misconduct accounts for about 67% of retracted medical papers (Fang et al. 2012); 3.8% of 20,000+ biomedical papers had problematic figures, roughly half of them showing signs of deliberate manipulation (Bik et al. 2016).

The research methods errors row is the one to read carefully as an economist. Reinhart and Rogoff’s spreadsheet excluded five countries and reversed a conclusion that shaped austerity policy (Herndon et al. 2014). Brodeur et al. (2023) found coding errors in about a quarter of economics papers that were re-executed. The deck’s core point is that error rates scale super-linearly with complexity: code is a network of dependencies, not a linear proof, so one upstream mistake flows into every downstream result.

Tier 2a: team-level analytic problems

Here nobody need be lying. The problems come from collective analytic decisions: a pipeline that silently breaks at a handoff between an RA and a PI, a team running many analyses and writing up the significant one, a hypothesis rewritten after the results came in, or a finding that only survives one of many defensible specifications. The evidence is striking because it is so ordinary: 58% of surveyed psychologists self-reported at least one questionable research practice (John et al. 2012); 70 teams analyzing the same brain-imaging data reached divergent conclusions on 5 of 9 hypotheses (Botvinik-Nezer et al. 2020); 29 teams analyzing the same referee data produced odds ratios from 0.89 to 2.93 (Silberzahn et al. 2018).

Publication bias appears here as a team decision on purpose. Franco et al. (2014) found that 68% of null results from pre-registered TESS survey experiments were never even written up — the file drawer is filled by researchers before any journal sees the work.

HARKing is the one row where the deck admits there is no automated detection at all (slide 30). A hypothesis invented after the fact looks identical to a genuine prediction in the final paper, so the only evidence is process evidence: a timestamped preregistration or preprint to compare against. That is why preregistration is the primary remedy rather than one option among many.

Tier 2b: group and collusion-level misconduct

These problems involve coordination across authors, editors, institutions, or journals, and the evidence lives in the relationships rather than in any one manuscript. Richardson et al. (2025) found that 0.25% of PLOS ONE editors handled 30.2% of its retracted articles, with the same concentrated pattern in Hindawi and IEEE proceedings. Wiley/Hindawi retracted more than 11,300 papers in 2022–24. The Problematic Paper Screener had flagged 32,786 suspected paper-mill products by 2024. Detection therefore uses network and clustering methods: citation network analysis, editor routing maps, and grouping papers that share an image bank.

Two rows here are subtle. Citation rings can be emergent rather than orchestrated — a prolific mentor’s former students naturally review one another — so the problem is hard to distinguish from a healthy research community without formal analysis. And institutional cover-up puts the investigator and the beneficiary in the same building: the university that receives the grant is the body asked to investigate its own star. ORI released only 2 findings in 2025, against an average of about 10 a year.

Tier 3: field and community-level problems

At this level the unit is an entire literature or the incentive system itself. The Open Science Collaboration (2015) replicated about 36–50% of 100 psychology findings, with replication effects on average half the original size; Camerer et al. (2018) found a 62% replication rate for economics lab experiments, with original effects roughly twice the replication effects. Mueller-Langer et al. (2019) found that fewer than 0.1% of articles in the top 50 economics journals from 1974 to 2014 were replications. Data disappear, too: availability declines about 17% per year after publication (Vines et al. 2014), and 79% of data promised in availability statements was unavailable on request (Gabelica et al. 2022).

Detection at this level means measuring the literature (p-curve, z-curve, TES, bias-corrected meta-analysis, replication projects) or the system (bibliometric audits, QRP surveys, FAIR scoring). Prevention is policy: DORA and the Leiden Manifesto on how researchers are evaluated, Registered Reports and outcomes-neutral review on what gets published, FAIR principles and funder mandates on what happens to data.

Publication bias appears twice, and the repetition is the point. The same literature-level tools (TES, p-curve, bias-corrected meta-analysis) appear in the Tier 2a and Tier 3 rows because a tool reads the distribution of published results, and that distribution does not reveal whether a null result was filed away by the team or rejected by the journal. Detection happens at the level of the literature; prevention has to target whoever made the decision.

Section three

Connections to what you already know

Three rows of the framework are problems you have already met in this course under econometric names.

The garden of forking paths is specification choice

Every regression you have run involved choices: which control variables to include, how to handle outliers, whether to log the outcome, which subsample to use, which fixed effects to add. Gelman and Loken called the full tree of such choices a “garden of forking paths.” The integrity problem is not that any one path is wrong. It is that a reader sees only the path that reached significance, with no idea how many equally defensible paths were never reported.

You already know why the control-variable branch matters: adding or dropping a correlated control moves the coefficient (omitted variable bias) and changes its standard error. The forking-paths guide’s classic example is pure OVB: studies of coffee and mortality that control for smoking tend to find a protective effect of coffee, while those that do not tend to find null or harmful effects.

The three Tier 2a tools each make the rest of the garden visible:

  • Multiverse analysis runs every combination of defensible choices. Three outlier rules × two operationalizations × three covariate sets × two subsamples is 3 × 2 × 3 × 2 = 36 specifications, and the result is a distribution of estimates rather than a single number.
  • Specification curve analysis sorts those estimates into a curve and adds permutation-based inference: is the pattern across all specifications stronger than you would see under the null?
  • Vibration of effects is the control-variable problem stated exactly. With k candidate covariates it fits every subset, 2k models in all (1,024 for k = 10), and reports how far the coefficient of interest moves. A ratio of largest to smallest absolute estimate above 2, or any sign change, signals fragility.

Economics has its own evidence. In the many-analysts study included in this folder (Huntington-Klein et al. 2021), seven replicators each re-did two published causal studies. No two reported the same sample size, significance varied across replications, and for one study even the sign varied. The standard deviation of estimates across analysts was 3–4 times the mean reported standard error.

The standard error measures sampling uncertainty given one specification. It says nothing about uncertainty across specifications. When the analyst-to-analyst spread is several times the reported SE, the confidence interval in any single paper badly understates how uncertain the finding really is. That is why pre-specified analysis plans and robustness tables are integrity tools, not formalities.

Publication bias is a selection problem that inflates effect sizes

If only significant results get published, the published record is a sample selected on the outcome. Small, imprecise studies reach significance only when they happen to draw a large estimate, so the published small studies overstate the effect and the literature’s average is biased upward. The replication projects measure this directly: replication effects were about half the original size in psychology (OSC 2015), and original effects were roughly twice the replication effects in economics lab experiments (Camerer et al. 2018).

This is also why the next unit on meta-analysis cannot simply average published studies. A pooled estimate absorbs the selection. The publication-bias tools are built on that insight:

  • Funnel plot and Egger test: plot each study’s effect against its precision. Without bias, the cloud is a symmetric funnel around the true effect; with bias, the corner where small null studies should be is empty. The Egger test turns the asymmetry into a regression test (conventionally at p < .10, and only with roughly 10 or more studies).
  • Trim-and-fill: imputes the missing mirror-image studies and re-estimates the pooled effect.
  • PET-PEESE: a weighted regression across studies. Its intercept is the predicted effect for a study with a standard error of zero — an infinitely large sample, which publication bias cannot touch.
  • Excess significance test: compares the number of significant results with the number the studies’ power says to expect.

PET-PEESE is the tool you can already read, because it is an ordinary regression with study i as the unit of observation:

dieffect size = β0effect at SE = 0 + β1 SEismall-study bias + eierror

Studies are weighted by 1 / SEi2, so precise studies count more. PET tests whether β0 differs from zero. If it does, PEESE re-runs the regression with SEi2 in place of SEi and reports its intercept as the corrected estimate. The slope β1 is the tell: in an unbiased literature, effect size should not depend on precision. The guide’s example: the published ego-depletion meta-analytic effect was d ≈ 0.62, the PEESE correction gave about 0.24, and a 23-lab preregistered replication found about 0.04 (Carter et al. 2015; Hagger et al. 2016). The correction was not exact, but it pointed firmly in the right direction.

Computational reproducibility is your own workflow

The research-methods-errors and pipeline-failure rows are about economics specifically. Brodeur et al. (2023) found coding errors in about 25% of re-executed economics papers; Chang and Li found only 14–43% of published economics studies computationally reproducible on a first independent attempt. The American Economic Association’s Data Editor (2018–) now requires replication packages to run cleanly before publication.

The remedies in those rows are the habits this course asks you to practice:

  • An R Markdown file that knits from raw data to final tables is a push-button reproducibility test. If it only works after you run a few chunks by hand in the console, it is not reproducible.
  • Never edit data by hand in a spreadsheet; every transformation belongs in code, which is the written provenance log the deck asks for.
  • Version control (Git), a pinned package environment (renv), and a pipeline tool (targets) are the team-scale versions of the same idea.
  • Gentzkow and Shapiro’s Code and Data for the Social Sciences is the practitioner guide the framework cites; Peikert et al.’s tutorial in this folder walks through an R-based reproducible workflow.

A replication package that has never been run on a clean machine has not been tested. Knitting your document from a fresh R session is the cheapest integrity check you will ever run.

Two further documents round out the toolkit. The compiled toolkit collects the table of contents and all the individual guides in one PDF. The closing chapter, “Looking Ahead: Scaling Open Science”, argues that the tools’ real bottleneck is data representation rather than computation: statistics are locked in inconsistently formatted PDFs, so running a p-curve on a hundred studies is weeks of hand extraction. It proposes formal ontologies — machine-readable vocabularies of study designs, measures, and effect sizes, already mature in biomedicine — as the infrastructure that would let these checks run automatically and at scale.

Section six

What should be clear in my mind?

If you can explain each of these in your own words, you have the deck.

  1. Problems are organized by the unit that produces them: individual, team, group, or field. The unit determines what evidence exists, which tools can see it, and who can fix it.
  2. Each problem runs Intro → Evidence of scope → Detection → Prevention. You should be able to fill in those four boxes for any row.
  3. Integrity covers honest error as well as fraud. Coding errors, broken pipelines and fragile specifications are in the framework for a reason.
  4. Most detection tools find inconsistency, not misconduct. Establishing intent takes process evidence and investigation.
  5. Forking paths are specification choices. Multiverse, specification curve and vibration of effects make the unreported choices — especially control variables — visible.
  6. Publication bias is selection on the outcome, and it inflates effect sizes. Funnel plots, Egger, trim-and-fill, PET-PEESE and TES detect or correct it, and the meta-analysis unit depends on them.
  7. Computational reproducibility is a property of your own workflow. A document that knits from raw data on a clean machine is the basic test the AEA Data Editor applies.

Key takeaways

  • Name the level before choosing the tool. GRIM cannot see a citation ring; network analysis cannot see an impossible mean.
  • Many of the worst numbers are about ordinary practice, not villains: 58% self-reported QRPs, 68% of null results never written up, 70 teams diverging on the same data.
  • Economics is not exempt. Coding errors in about a quarter of re-executed papers, 14–43% computational reproducibility, and fewer than 0.1% of top-journal articles being replications.
  • The SE understates real uncertainty when analyst-to-analyst variation is several times larger than sampling variation.
  • Publication bias is found in the literature but prevented at the decision — the team’s file drawer and the journal’s acceptance rule.
  • HARKing has no statistical detector; only a timestamped plan can reveal it.
  • Collusion leaves its trace in relationships, so detection needs networks and metadata rather than a closer read of one paper.
  • Field-level problems need field-level remedies: how researchers are evaluated, what journals accept, and whether data survive.
  • The tools’ bottleneck is data representation. Machine-readable reporting would let these checks run at scale.
