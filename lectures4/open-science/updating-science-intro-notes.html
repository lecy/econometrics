Part IV · Introduction

Updating Science in the Modern Era

An overview for Part IV, Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis. Between about 2005 and 2015, researchers began checking published findings at scale and learned that many did not hold up. What followed was the fastest period of methodological reform in modern science. This note covers what the early checks found, why it happened, and what has been built since.

Section one

Three ways to check a finding

The field now keeps these apart, because each one fails for different reasons and calls for a different fix.

TermThe testWhat a failure looks likeTypical cause
ReproducibilitySame data, same code, rerunThe published numbers do not come backMissing files, broken code, undisclosed cleaning steps, data never shared
ReplicabilityNew data, same design and questionA different answer, usually a smaller or null effectLow power, publication bias, p-hacking, a false positive
RobustnessSame data, a different but defensible analysisThe conclusion flips under reasonable alternative choicesAnalytic flexibility (the “garden of forking paths”)

These definitions follow the 2019 National Academies report Reproducibility and Replicability in Science: reproducibility is computational, replicability is empirical. Some economists still say “replication” for the computational check, so watch for it in the readings.

Section two

The wake-up call: what the early studies found

The failure rates were not at the margin. They were large, and they showed up in field after field.

The argument came first on paper. In 2005, John Ioannidis showed from first principles that in a field with small samples, flexible analysis and long-shot hypotheses, most statistically significant findings will be false. Over the next decade, industry labs and coordinated replication teams put that claim to an empirical test.

Four patterns that shaped everything after

  • About half held up. Replication rates clustered near 50% across very different fields, and surviving effects were about half their published size.
  • The failures were predictable. In Camerer’s projects, peers betting in prediction markets forecast which studies would fail with reasonable accuracy. The weak studies were readable from the papers themselves.
  • “Hidden moderators” were not the explanation. Running the same study in 36 countries made almost no difference to whether it replicated.
  • The file drawer was real. Because Franco’s team knew every study that had been funded, they could show that null results mostly went unwritten, not merely unpublished.

Why this is a call to action, not a reason for cynicism. These numbers exist because scientists went looking for them. Large teams spent years rerunning other people’s studies, and journals like Science and Nature published the results. That is science correcting itself. Some scholars, notably Fanelli (2018), argue the word “crisis” overstates the case. Most agree on the narrower claim: published effects were too large, uncertainty was understated, and the system needed to change.

Section three

Why it happened

Mostly honest researchers responding sensibly to a system that rewarded the wrong thing.

Fraud is real, but it is the smallest source of unreliable findings. Most of the problem came from three places:

  • Low statistical power. When a study has only a 20% chance of detecting a true effect, the estimates that clear the significance bar are, by construction, the ones that came out too large. That one fact explains most of the “effects shrink by half” pattern. Median power in neuroscience was near 20% (Button et al., 2013).
  • Analytic flexibility. Which outliers to drop, which controls to add, when to stop collecting data. Each choice is defensible on its own, but choices made after seeing the data turn a 1-in-20 fluke into a publishable result. When 29 teams were given the same soccer data and the same question, their estimated odds ratios ran from 0.89 to 2.93 (Silberzahn et al., 2018).
  • Incentives. Journals rewarded positive, novel results; careers ran on publication counts; replications earned little credit (about 1% of psychology articles); and no one was paid to rerun anyone else’s code. Smaldino and McElreath (2016) called the result “the natural selection of bad science.”

Because the cause was mostly the system and not the people in it, the fix could be mostly systemic too. That is the good news of the past fifteen years.

Section four

How science responded

One organizing idea: make the decisions that produce false positives visible, and make them before the data arrive.

The reform movement started with a handful of researchers and journal editors and grew into new institutions: the Center for Open Science (2013), the Institute for Replication (2022), national reproducibility networks, and a new research field, metascience, that studies how science itself works.

What made the response work

  • It targeted timing, not honesty. A preregistration does not forbid dropping an outlier. It forbids deciding to drop it after seeing that doing so makes the result significant. Because the reforms don’t presume anyone is cheating, careful researchers could adopt them without taking offense.
  • It made verification somebody’s job. Before 2015, no one was paid to rerun a paper’s code. Now data editors at the AEA, AJPS and Management Science do exactly that before publication.
  • It made correction public. Retraction Watch (2010) and PubPeer (2012) created a public record, and retracted papers are now flagged automatically in Zotero and Crossref.

Section five

Evidence of progress

Where the reforms are used, they work, and the gains are large.

Economics reproducibility

33%→85%+

Chang & Li (2015), versus Brodeur et al. (Nature, 2026) on economics and political science papers. Median reanalyzed effect: 99% of the published one.

Management Science

12%→95%

Share of papers sharing anything before the data policy, versus the share reproduced once data were accessible under it (Fisar et al., 2024).

Positive results

96%→44%

Standard psychology papers versus Registered Reports. A 44% rate is what an honest literature testing real hypotheses looks like (Scheel et al., 2021).

Data sharing

<3%→~40%

At Psychological Science, within two years of introducing open-data badges (Kidwell et al., 2016).

  • Pre-analysis plans reduce p-hacking. Across nearly 16,000 test statistics from economics RCTs, studies with a full pre-analysis plan show less suspicious bunching just above the significance threshold (Brodeur et al., 2024).
  • Registered Reports are rated as more rigorous. When 353 researchers blind-rated them against matched standard papers, the Registered Reports scored higher on rigor and quality (Soderberg et al., 2021).
  • Fraud is caught faster. The Surgisphere COVID papers fell within weeks. High-profile cases in behavioral science were exposed by outsiders using forensic tools that did not exist in 2010.
  • The culture has shifted. A single significant result from a small sample no longer carries the weight it did in 2010. Readers now ask about power, preregistration and data availability before believing a claim, and the vocabulary of p-hacking, HARKing and the file drawer is standard in methods courses, including this one.

Section six

The work ahead

The reforms work where they are used. The next task is using them more widely.

  • Adoption is still thin. Registered Reports are offered by about 7% of psychology journals and under 1% elsewhere. Many preregistrations are loose enough that authors deviate from them without saying so.
  • Incentives have moved only at the margin. Publication counts still drive careers, and replications are still a small share of what gets published.
  • Data access sets the ceiling. When data cannot be shared, reproducibility drops to about 68%, and current reforms have little to say about confidential or proprietary data.
  • Observational social science has the furthest to go. The fields with the longest chains of analytic choices, which includes most applied econometrics, adopted the fewest reforms.
  • Even the reformers need checking. A 2023 paper claiming that rigor-enhancing practices yield high replicability was retracted in 2024, after critics showed its central analysis was not preregistered as stated. The system caught it, which is the point.

Where you come in. The most effective reforms were not imposed from above. They were habits that individual researchers adopted and then made normal: writing down an analysis plan before looking at the data, reporting every specification you ran, sharing code that runs on someone else’s machine. You can do all of these on your next project. Part IV gives you the tools.
