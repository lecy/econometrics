Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Research Fraud and the Reproducibility Crisis

A reading guide for the opening lecture of Unit 22 — how often published research turns out to be wrong or fake, how people catch it, why the system keeps producing it, and why every tool you learned in the regression units is part of the story.

Section one

Key concepts

The vocabulary and the core claims of the deck, each stated as a claim.

Key concepts

Fraud, questionable practice and honest error are different things that produce the same symptom: a published result that is not true.

  • Fabrication = inventing data or results (Schön, Stapel, Surgisphere).
  • Falsification = manipulating real data, images or analyses so they show something they do not (image splicing, altered spreadsheets).
  • Questionable research practices = legal-looking analytic choices made after seeing the data — dropping observations, adding controls, stopping data collection when p < .05. Collectively called p-hacking.
  • Honest error = spreadsheet mistakes, coding bugs, mislabelled samples.

The detection tools in this deck flag inconsistencies. Which of the four produced them is a separate question.

Reproducibility and replicability are different tests.

  • Reproducible = same data, same code, same numbers. A computational check.
  • Replicable = new data, same design, same conclusion. A scientific check.

A study can pass the first and fail the second. Fraud usually fails the first as soon as anyone asks for the data.

Retractions are growing much faster than publications.

  • About 22% compound annual growth in retractions versus about 6.25% for publications — roughly 3.5 times faster.
  • Around 14,000 retractions in 2023; 63,000+ records in the Retraction Watch Database.

But a retraction is a detection event, not a fraud event. Part of the growth is better policing.

Most fraud detection is arithmetic on published summary statistics.

  • GRIM — is this mean possible with this many integer responses?
  • SPRITE — can any raw dataset produce this mean, SD, N and range?
  • statcheck — does the reported p-value match the reported test statistic?
  • Image forensics — are these figures duplicated or edited?

The publishing economy now pays for volume.

Article processing charges (APCs) mean journals earn more by accepting more. Paper mills sell authorship; fake peer review lets manuscripts through the gate. The deck’s point is that this is an industry, not a handful of bad actors.

Incentives make misconduct rational, which is why reforms target the incentives.

publication counts → careers & funding → pressure for novel, significant results → p-hacking, publication bias, fraud

Preregistration, registered reports, open data and post-publication review each cut one link in that chain.

For an econometrician, the reproducibility crisis is mostly about standard tools used badly.

  • The 0.05 threshold is what gets hacked.
  • Specification choices (controls, samples, functional form) are where the flexibility lives.
  • Low power plus publication bias guarantees that published effects are inflated.

Section two

The scale of the problem

What the retraction numbers show — and what they cannot show.

Retractions: exponential, not linear

Slide 3 plots annual retractions from 2000 to 2023. The curve starts near 140 a year and ends near 14,000. That is a hundredfold increase in 23 years, which is what 22% compound growth produces:

140 × 1.2223≈14,000

Publications grew too, but at about 6.25% a year. The ratio of the two rates is where the deck’s headline “3.5× faster” comes from (22 / 6.25 ≈ 3.5). Note the dip around 2020 and the spike after it; the 2022–23 surge is dominated by mass retractions of paper-mill products (Section four).

A data caution. Slide 3 also says the 14,000 retractions of 2023 equal “all retractions from 2020–22 combined.” The slide’s own chart puts 2020 at about 7,000 and 2022 at about 11,000, so that comparison does not hold as stated. The growth rate and the order of magnitude are the claims to keep.

Slide 4 reports retractions per 10,000 papers (Web of Science, 2000–2024):

Two things to notice. First, the social sciences are not a safe corner: at 38 per 10,000 they sit alongside the biomedical fields. Second, the top of the table is driven by fake peer review and paper mills rather than by lone fabricators. Clinical and life sciences have more than 21,000 retractions in total, and there the cost is not just wasted citations but patient harm.

Correction. The slide describes the computer-science rate as “10× higher than Physics.” Using the slide’s own chart values (157.1 and 3.25), the ratio is about 48×. The slide also gives an overall average of 11 per 10,000 across 42 million papers, which sits below every field except physics; the field rates and the average probably come from different denominators, so compare fields with each other rather than with the average.

Is fraud rising, or is detection improving?

The deck is careful on this point, and so should you be. Its own first takeaway (slide 15) is that rising retractions reflect better detection, not only more fraud. Look at the parallel timeline on slide 6: Retraction Watch starts tracking in 2010, GRIM and statcheck appear in 2016, SPRITE and PubPeer scale up in 2017–18, AI image screening arrives in 2024–25. Every one of those raises the retraction count even if the underlying rate of misconduct were flat.

This is a measurement problem you already know. A retraction count is an outcome that depends on two things — the true rate of bad papers and the probability that a bad paper is caught:

retractions=bad papers×Pr(detected)×Pr(journal acts)

A rising product does not tell you which term is rising. The same logic cuts the other way: slide 14 notes that the US Office of Research Integrity issued only 2 misconduct findings in 2025, against an average of about 10 a year. Fewer findings there means weaker enforcement, not less misconduct. Treat every count in this deck as a lower bound on a quantity you cannot observe directly.

High-profile cases and the detection toolkit

Who got caught, and the (mostly simple) tools that caught them.

Notice who did the catching. Wansink was exposed by outside methodologists running SPRITE; Gino by the Data Colada bloggers; the Tessier-Lavigne, Gino and Dana-Farber cases were flagged on PubPeer. Institutions mostly acted after outsiders had done the work. Notice too that prestige offered no protection: Nature, Science, The Lancet, Harvard and Stanford all appear.

GRIM: the granularity of a mean

The GRIM test (Brown & Heathers) is the cleanest example of fraud detection as arithmetic. If N people each give an integer answer (a 1–7 scale, a count), their sum is an integer, so the mean must be a multiple of 1/N:

x̄= integerN

With N = 20, possible means move in steps of 0.05: … 3.40, 3.45, 3.50 … A reported mean of 3.47 cannot come from 20 integer responses. It is a typo, a miscounted sample, excluded cases that were not reported — or invented numbers. GRIM only works when N is small relative to the reporting precision (with two decimals, below about 100), which is exactly the small-sample world where p-hacking is most effective.

SPRITE, statcheck, images and tortured phrases

  • SPRITE (Sample Parameter Reconstruction via Iterative Techniques) extends GRIM. Given a mean, SD, N and range, it searches for raw datasets that could have produced them. If the only feasible datasets are absurd, the summary statistics are suspect. It is how Wansink’s data were shown to require, in the deck’s example, a child eating 60 carrots.
  • statcheck reads a PDF, extracts each reported test (e.g. t(28) = 1.20, p < .05), recomputes the p-value from the statistic and degrees of freedom, and flags mismatches. In that illustrative case the true two-sided p is about 0.24; a mismatch that flips the conclusion is a “gross error.”
  • Image forensics. Elisabeth Bik and colleagues screened biomedical papers by eye and found problematic duplicated figures in 3.8% of them (Bik et al., 2016). Her volunteer work has led to 1,331 retractions by 2024. Tools such as Imagetwin and Proofig now automate the screening at submission.
  • PubPeer is an open post-publication comment platform where concerns are posted and coordinated — often before any institution acts.
  • Tortured phrases. Paper mills disguise plagiarism with synonym software, so “artificial neural network” becomes “counterfeit brain organization.” The Problematic Paper Screener searches for these fingerprints and has flagged 32,000+ suspected paper-mill products.

Read the deck’s own caveat. GRIM, SPRITE and statcheck detect inconsistencies, not fraud. Most statcheck flags are typos. Confidence comes from several independent tools pointing at the same paper — and, decisively, from the raw data. This is why open data appears in the reform list: it turns detective work into a reproducibility check.

Open science as preventive infrastructure

Slide 9 groups the prevention tools into three families:

  • Preregistration — a time-stamped plan of hypotheses, methods and analyses filed before data collection (OSF hosts 500,000+). The deck is honest that the evidence is mixed: preregistered studies have larger samples and more power analyses, but not necessarily fewer positive results. The stronger version is the registered report, where peer review happens before data exist and acceptance does not depend on the result.
  • Open data and code, following the FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable), and the 2013 US OSTP mandate for federally funded research.
  • Post-publication review — PubPeer, the Retraction Watch Database, social media, and volunteer sleuths.

Section four

The publishing economy and the incentives behind it

Why misconduct is a market outcome and not only a character flaw.

From gatekeeping to pay-to-publish

Under the subscription model, readers paid and journals made money by being selective. Under open access with article processing charges, authors pay (roughly $2,000 to $15,000+ per paper) and journals make money by publishing. Slide 11 lays out the resulting spectrum:

TierWho paysQuality gate
Subscription journalsReaderRigorous but slow peer review.
Hybrid / gold OAAuthor (APC)Varies. Very profitable: Elsevier reported about $3.6B profit in 2023.
Grey-zone OAAuthorFast, weak review; broad scope; high acceptance rates.
Predatory journalsAuthorPeer review in name only; acceptance follows payment.
Paper mills / fake journals“Author” buys a slotNone. Fabricated or AI-generated manuscripts; hijacked ISSNs.

The key fact is that the bottom of the spectrum leaks into the top. More than 11,300 papers were retracted from Wiley’s Hindawi journals in 2022–24 after paper mills infiltrated them; at PLOS ONE, 0.25% of editors handled 30.2% of retracted articles. A handful of compromised editors is enough. That is why the deck concludes that journal reputation is no longer a reliable proxy for integrity.

Paper mills and fake peer review

  • Richardson and colleagues (2025, PNAS) estimate that the number of fraudulent papers may double every 1.5 years; the deck cites estimates that 5–15% of biomedical papers may be fraudulent or suspicious.
  • Authorship is for sale at roughly $500–$3,000 per paper.
  • Fake peer review — authors suggesting reviewers whose email addresses they control, or mills impersonating real academics — accounts for about half of all retractions, according to the deck.
  • Reviewer burnout makes this easy: editors short of reviewers accept suggested ones.

Incentives at three levels

Slide 13 organizes the incentives by level. The pattern is that each level rewards counts and novelty, and none rewards being right.

LevelPressure
SystemicNational research assessment tied to publication counts (UK REF, China’s evaluation system); NIH success rates near 20%; the journal impact factor as a proxy for career worth.
InstitutionalTenure and promotion require high-impact publications; grant overhead funds university budgets; early-career jobs depend on paper counts.
IndividualStapel: “I wanted too much, too fast.” Paper mills offer a market solution. Amaral describes a situation where the optimal strategy is to start cheating to win.

Slide 14 adds the AI arms race: 14% of 2024 biomedical abstracts show signs of AI-generated text, generative models can produce convincing western blots, and detection tools are racing to keep up — while enforcement capacity (ORI’s 2 findings in 2025) is shrinking.

The economist’s reading. When the payoff to a publication is high, the probability of being caught is low, and the penalty is rarely applied, misconduct is not irrational — it is a predictable response to the payoff structure. That is why the serious reforms change the payoffs (what gets counted, what gets published) rather than just adding ethics training.

Section five

Why an econometrics student should care

Outright fraud is rare. The tools you have been learning are how most wrong results get made — and caught.

p-hacking exploits the threshold you already know

In the program-impact lecture you learned that “significant” only means the confidence interval excludes zero, and that the p-value is the widest interval you can draw before it contains the null. p-hacking is simply the search for a version of the analysis in which that interval clears zero: drop an outlier, add a control, split a subgroup, stop collecting data at the right moment. Each choice looks defensible alone. Tried together, they push the real false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%. The telltale sign in a literature is a pile-up of p-values just below .05.

Low power plus publication bias guarantees inflated effects

The Economist’s “Trouble at the lab” works the arithmetic. Suppose 1,000 hypotheses are tested, 100 of which are true, with power 0.8 and α = 0.05. You get 80 true positives and 45 false positives (5% of 900). The share of “significant” findings that are false is

4545 + 80 ≈0.36

Cut power to 0.4 and it becomes 45 / (45 + 40) ≈ 0.53: more than half the positive results are wrong. Now add publication bias — only the significant results get printed — and a second problem appears. In a low-powered study, an estimate only clears the significance bar when sampling error happened to push it upward, so the published effect sizes are systematically too large. This is the bridge to Unit 24: a meta-analysis that averages a filtered literature averages inflated numbers.

Analytic flexibility is specification choice

Every regression unit asked you to make choices: which controls, which functional form, which sample, how to code a variable, where to draw the bandwidth in an RD. Those are the “researcher degrees of freedom” the reform movement is worried about. Huntington-Klein et al. (2021) measured how much they matter. Seven economists each independently replicated two published applied-micro studies — one on compulsory schooling and teen births, one an RD on Medicare eligibility at 65 and self-employment — from the same instructions and public data. No two replicators ended up with the same sample size; statistical significance varied across replications, and in one study the sign did too. The standard deviation of the estimates across replicators was 3 to 4 times the average reported standard error.

The lesson for reading a regression table. The standard error measures sampling variability for one analyst’s specification. It says nothing about the variability across reasonable analysts, which in this study was larger. Specification curves, multiverse analyses and preregistered analysis plans are attempts to put that hidden variance back on the page.

Confounding and data you cannot see

The Surgisphere Lancet paper reported in-hospital mortality of 23.8% for COVID-19 patients given hydroxychloroquine with an antibiotic, against 9.3% for controls, across 671 hospitals. Before anyone questioned whether the data existed, critics raised a textbook omitted variable problem: physicians give experimental drugs to sicker patients, and the database had no good measure of severity. Then came the plausibility checks — more Australian deaths than the country had officially recorded, thousands of African patients with detailed electronic records — and the refusal to share hospital-level data. Both lines of attack, bad identification and unverifiable data, are the ones this course has trained you to run.

Economics is not exempt

The Economist piece recalls Thomas Herndon, a graduate student who tried to replicate Reinhart and Rogoff’s influential finding on public debt and growth and found errors, including a spreadsheet error. The replication was possible only because the data could be obtained. That is the ordinary, non-fraudulent version of this lecture’s problem, and the reason the next lectures stress reproducible workflows.

Reading the popular accounts

The pubs/ folder holds five readings that tell the story from different angles, plus a news report on Surgisphere. Read at least the first two.

  • “Trouble at the lab” (The Economist, 2013). The best single overview. Industry scientists at Amgen could reproduce only 6 of 53 landmark cancer studies, and Bayer about a quarter of 67; a spoof paper was accepted by 157 of 304 journals; in surveys, 2% of scientists admit fabricating or falsifying data while 28% know of colleagues using questionable practices. Its worked example on power and false positives is the one used above.
  • “Psychology’s replication crisis is real, Many Labs 2 says” (Ed Yong, The Atlantic, 2018). About 200 psychologists re-ran 28 famous studies with 15,305 participants in 36 countries — on average 60 times the original samples — and replicated 14. Crucially, results barely varied across sites, which undercuts the “hidden moderator” defence that failed replications were run on different people.
  • “When the revolution came for Amy Cuddy” (Susan Dominus, New York Times Magazine, 2017). The human side. Cuddy’s 2010 power-pose study and TED talk made her famous; a larger, double-blind replication by Eva Ranehill (200 subjects) did not find the hormone or risk-taking effects; co-author Dana Carney later said she did not believe the effects were real. The article also explains p-hacking through Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn’s “False-Positive Psychology” demonstration, which used standard methods to “show” that listening to a Beatles song made people younger. Read it for how norms changed under researchers who had followed the old rules.
  • “The influence of hidden researcher decisions in applied microeconomics” (Huntington-Klein et al., Economic Inquiry, 2021). The many-analysts study described above; the one reading written by and for economists.
  • “Reproducible research in R” (Peikert, van Lissa & Brandmaier, Psych, 2021). The practical response. It names four threats to computational reproducibility (multiple versions, copy-and-paste errors, undocumented order of computation, ambiguous software dependencies) and four remedies: version control (Git), dynamic documents (R Markdown), dependency tracking (Make) and software management (Docker), wrapped in their repro package. It also proposes “preregistration as code.”
  • “A mysterious company’s coronavirus papers in top medical journals may be unraveling” (Servick & Enserink, Science, 2020). Written on the day The Lancet and NEJM issued expressions of concern, before the retractions. Useful as a real-time record of how outside researchers spotted the problems.

Section six

Reforms, and how to read the slides

What is being done, and a map of the deck.

Reform efforts underway

  • Stockholm Declaration (2025) — 34 specific actions, led by abandoning publication counting and valuing quality over quantity in hiring and tenure (Sabel & Larhammar, 2025).
  • Registered reports — peer review before data collection, acceptance regardless of results. The one reform that removes publication bias by design.
  • Open data mandates from NIH, NSF, the Gates Foundation and most major funders.
  • United2Act — publishers sharing intelligence on paper mills.
  • Elisabeth Bik Science Integrity Fund (2025, with Retraction Watch) — money for the volunteer sleuths who currently do much of the detection.

Map each reform onto the incentive chain from Section one. Registered reports and open data change what gets published and make checking cheap; the Stockholm Declaration changes what gets counted; the sleuth fund pays for detection. None relies on persuading individuals to be more virtuous.

Section seven

What should be clear in my mind?

A self-check before moving on to Unit 23.

  1. I can distinguish fabrication, falsification, questionable research practices and honest error, and explain why detection tools cannot tell them apart on their own.
  2. I can explain why a rising retraction count does not prove rising fraud — retractions multiply the true rate by the probability of detection and action.
  3. I can run a GRIM check by hand and say what statcheck and SPRITE recompute.
  4. I can describe how APCs, paper mills and fake peer review change what gets published, and why journal reputation is a weaker signal than it was.
  5. I can connect p-hacking to the 0.05 threshold and publication bias to inflated effect sizes, using the power arithmetic.
  6. I can explain what Huntington-Klein et al. found and why the reported standard error understates total uncertainty.
  7. I can match each reform to the incentive it targets.

Key takeaways

  • Retractions grow about 3.5× faster than publications, but part of that is better detection.
  • Most detection is arithmetic on reported statistics; open data turns it into a direct check.
  • Prestige does not protect. Landmark cases ran through Nature, Science, The Lancet, Harvard and Stanford.
  • Pay-to-publish economics and paper mills make fraud an industry, one that infiltrates legitimate journals.
  • Incentives reward counts and novelty, which makes misconduct and p-hacking predictable responses.
  • Low power plus publication bias inflates published effects — the central problem for the meta-analysis unit that follows.
  • Specification choices are a hidden source of variance that can exceed the reported standard error.
  • Preregistration, registered reports, open data and post-publication review are the working defences.
