Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Replicating Yeung, Yay, and Feldman: From Search to Data

Yeung, Yay, and Feldman (2022) preregistered a meta-analysis of the omission bias and posted everything on the OSF: the search terms, every article they read, every exclusion, and the coded statistics. It all lives in one Excel workbook. This guide walks through that workbook tab by tab, shows how each tab records one step of the review, and rebuilds it as eight tidy tables. The analysis guide picks up where this one ends.

Section one

Key concepts

What a reproducible literature review has to record.

Key concepts

A meta-analysis is a funnel, and every step narrows it.

2,570 search hits + 14 from authors → 124 records → 76 full texts → 13 articles → 21 samples → 49 effect sizes

Each arrow is a set of decisions. A reader can only check the result if every decision is written down where it can be found.

The unit of analysis changes as you go down the funnel.

  • Search and screening work with articles.
  • Coding works with studies inside articles, then samples inside studies.
  • The analysis works with effect sizes: one comparison of action and inaction on one measure.

A good workbook keeps one table per unit and a shared key that links them.

Excluded rows are data too.

The authors coded 80 effect rows that they later dropped, and kept them in their own tab with a reason for each. That tab is what lets you disagree with a decision instead of just trusting it.

A 155-column coding sheet mixes three kinds of information.

Who was studied and how (descriptors), the numbers the paper reported (statistics), and the categories used to explain variation (moderators). Splitting them makes each one readable and makes errors visible.

Section three

Stage 1: The literature search

How the authors found 2,584 candidate records.

The search ran in two rounds on Google Scholar. The first round used eight narrow queries, mostly “omission bias” paired with one outcome term. Its purpose was to learn the vocabulary of the field and the names of the people working in it. What it turned up shaped the final query, which ORs together every label for the effect and every pair of names for the contrast (action/inaction, doing/allowing, active/passive), ANDs that with the outcomes of interest, and removes trolley problems.

What the paper reports

Method, pp. 1502–1503, and Figure 1: the final query returned 2,570 records. The authors also scanned the reference lists of found articles, used Scholar’s “related articles” and “cited by” links, and asked for published and unpublished work by e-mail, on ResearchGate, on Twitter, and on mailing lists, once in 2017 and again in 2020. That call produced 14 articles the search had missed. After removing duplicates, 124 records remained.

Three things to notice. First, the hit counts from the first round do not add up to anything; they overlap heavily and were never meant to be screened. Second, the tab records no search dates, and Google Scholar results change over time, so the exact 2,570 cannot be reproduced today. The paper’s preregistration and the year of each call are the best time stamps available. Third, the step from 2,584 hits to 124 records (and then the 48 abstracts excluded at the next step) is not in the workbook at all. The paper trail begins at the full-text stage.

Section four

Stage 2: Screening the article list

76 full texts, one row each, judged against the inclusion criteria.

The Article list tab has one row per article that survived the abstract screen: 76 of them, matching the “full text articles evaluated for eligibility” box in Figure 1. Each row has the citation, the abstract, keywords, the number of studies, and the coder’s decision in a column called Included?. Four columns meant to record the search (serial number, date, search terms, and database) were never filled in; we dropped them.

What the paper reports: the criteria

pp. 1503–1504. Include an article if the independent variable is a clear experimental contrast between action and inaction with the same consequences (B1), and the dependent variable is a judgment of morality or blame or a decision between them (B2). Exclude correlational and one-sample designs (C1); studies of the action effect on regret, status quo bias, or norm theory (C2); studies without the statistics needed for an effect size, after asking the authors (C3); and trolley-style dilemmas that trade harm to one person against harm to several (C4).

Included? records only this screen. Twenty articles passed it, but only 13 made it into the analysis, and nothing in the tab says what happened to the other seven. We added a column, outcome, by looking each article up in the later tabs. The table has four outcomes: 13 articles included, 13 dropped after some of their effects were coded (the Excluded tab), 44 dropped at full text with a written reason (Coding decisions), and 6 dropped at full text with no reason recorded anywhere. Scroll right to see the abstracts the screeners read.

ASpranca et al. (1991), article 4. The classic study. Included? = Yes and Number of studies = 6. Only two of the six experiments survive, and they contribute two effect sizes. The other four are dropped in the Coding decisions tab.

BHaidt and Baron (1996), article 5. Relevant, and central to two of the hypotheses, but Included? = No. Its reason is in Coding decisions: no standard deviations, t values, or d for the action–inaction contrast. That is criterion C3.

CBrown et al. (2010), article 28. Passed the screen, and 22 effect rows were fully coded before the team decided the DV was a rating of disease and vaccine characteristics, not a moral judgment. Look for it in the Excluded tab.

Section five

Stage 3: From articles to studies to effects

The Number of studies column is where the unit of analysis changes.

An article can hold several experiments, an experiment can have several samples, and a sample can be measured on several DVs or under several scenarios. The workbook identifies each effect by five keys: article, study number, sample number, subgroup, and DV number. The authors’ R code builds its unit of analysis, which it calls articlestudy, from four of them.

ArticleStudies in articleStudies keptSamplesEffect rowsWhy so many rows
Spranca et al. (1991)6222One DV per study
Hayashi (2015)1136Three age groups × two scenarios
DeScioli et al. (2012)31112Twelve moral scenarios, from littering to cannibalism, rated by one sample
Jamison et al. (2020)1114Two DVs × two ways of forming the comparison groups

The last column is the key point for the analysis. Twelve DeScioli rows are not twelve studies; they are twelve correlated measurements of one sample of about 150 people. The paper’s count of 21 samples and 49 effects exists because the workbook records both levels, and the analysis has to model them (see the analysis guide, Section 2).

Section six

Stage 4: Coding decisions

Whole articles and single experiments dropped at full text, each with a written reason.

The Coding decisions tab is a log. Each row names an article and says what was dropped and why. Some rows drop a whole article (“All experiments of this article refer to Trolley problem”); others drop one experiment from an article that stays in. The last twelve rows cover articles that came in through the call to authors, which the workbook marks with a separator row; we turned that separator into the source column.

ADecisions 1–4: Spranca et al. Experiment 2 compares intentions, Experiment 3 is a trolley problem, and Experiment 6 tests a mechanism, so all three are dropped. Decision 3 is not an exclusion at all. It records a reading of the paper (“they combined Case 1 and Case 2”) that explains how the kept study was coded. Logs like this mix exclusions with coding notes, which is one reason a free-text reason column needs a reader.

BDecision 13: Haidt and Baron (1996). The whole article goes for missing statistics. The paper uses this as its example of criterion C3 (p. 1504).

Section seven

Stage 5: Effect rows coded, then excluded

80 rows from 17 articles that were fully coded before being dropped.

The Excluded tab uses nearly the same columns as the main coding tab, plus one at the front: the reason for exclusion. These are effects that looked eligible until someone coded them in detail. Their reasons are free text, so we added a criterion column that maps each reason onto the paper’s criteria. That mapping is ours, not the authors’, and you should check it.

CBrown et al. (2010), row X36. The first of 22 coded rows, all dropped for the same reason. Coding first and deciding later costs time, but it leaves a record: a reader who thinks vaccine-risk ratings are a decision measure can put these rows back and rerun the analysis.

DDeScioli et al. (2011), row X03. The same article that contributes four included effects also has eight excluded rows. Here the DV is punishment handed out in an economic game, not a moral judgment. Punishment is related to blame, and the coders judged it a different construct. This is a judgment call the tab makes visible.

Note that the Excluded tab counts rows, not articles. Figure 1 reports the 63 full-text exclusions by article (C1: 2, C2: 31, C3: 7, C4: 8, B1–B2: 15), and the workbook has no column that reproduces that tally. Our outcome column in the article list gets the total right (76 − 13 = 63) but not the breakdown.

Section eight

Stage 6: Coding the effect sizes

One 155-column tab, split into three tables that share the key effect_id.

The Study effect codings tab has 49 data rows below a three-row banner and a header. Its 155 columns fall into blocks: identifiers, sample demographics, design, independent variable, conditions, dependent variable, then three blocks of statistics (one each for scale DVs, counts, and proportions), a summary of conversions, and the moderator codes. The proportions block is completely empty. Many columns were filled in only when they applied, so most cells in the statistics blocks are blank.

We gave each row an ID in sheet order (E01–E49) and split the tab three ways.

Descriptors: who, where, and what design

Everything that describes the sample and the measure rather than the result. We collapsed three yes/no columns (DV proportions?, DV count?, DV scale?) into one dv_format column, and dropped the coder names, coding dates, and the empty gender columns.

1E01, Spranca et al. Study 1. Within-subject: 38 students rated both versions of each story. The action condition column shows the original design had two commission conditions (“before” and “after” the victim chooses) that the coders collapsed into one; the Collapse note column says so.

2E39, Jamison et al. (2020). A between-subject replication of the same story with 313 online participants. Same scenario, different design: the analysis guide shows the effect is less than half the size.

Statistics: the numbers each effect size is built from

This is the table the analysis runs on. In place of the workbook's three statistics blocks it has one type column, dv_format (scale, counts, or proportions, as the coders classified the DV). We added a second, es_input, that records which statistic actually becomes the effect size. The authors’ code takes group means and SDs when both conditions have them, a chi-square when there are counts, and otherwise the coders’ hand-computed best_estimate. The two columns do not always agree: Baron and Miller’s choices are coded as a scale DV but analyzed from a chi-square.

1E39: group means. M, SD, and n for both conditions. The code computes a standardized mean difference directly, and it matches the d = 0.45 the paper printed.

2E01: a t statistic. Only t(37) = 8.03 is reported. The coders divided by √N (the paired-data formula) and wrote 1.30 in best_estimate; the conversion column shows the arithmetic.

3E34: a chi-square. χ²(1) = 8.53. The coders’ note says “sample size 30,” but n is 50, and the code uses 50. The best_estimate of 1.26 and the value analyzed (0.89) disagree for that reason.

4E22: one comparison, three candidate effect sizes. DeScioli et al. (2012) report means, SDs, and a paired t(142) = 7.81 for the same comparison. The sheet’s best_estimate is 2t/√df = 1.31; the paired formula t/√N would give 0.65; and because means and SDs are present, the code uses them and gets 0.38. The analysis guide shows what this choice does to the pooled estimate.

5E49: a reversal. Connolly and Reb’s parents mostly chose to vaccinate, the opposite of an omission bias. A chi-square carries no sign, so the coders set reverse = Yes and the effect enters as negative.

Moderators: the codes that explain variation

Each moderator code sits next to a free-text explanation, which is what makes the coding auditable. Blank familiarity and responsibility codes mean “undetermined”; the analysis treats them as missing, except in MetaForest, which needs a value and gets the label “undetermined.”

1E49, Connolly and Reb. Parents deciding for their own child: familiar with the target and responsible for it. Only seven units carry the role-responsibility code, and this one is both the largest (N = 293) and the only strongly negative effect. When the analysis finds that the bias vanishes under role responsibility, this row carries much of that result.

2E46, Hayashi (2015). The “selfish” scenario, in which the actor gains. It is coded self as target, and it is one of only four self-target units in the whole meta-analysis, which is why the paper treats the self-vs.-other result as exploratory.

Section nine

Sanitizing

What we removed from the public workbook, and why.

The OSF workbook is public, but it was built as a working file, not a data release. Before putting a copy on a course site we removed anything a replication does not need:

  • Contact details. The Authors tab lists the name, e-mail addresses, affiliation, and personal web page of 91 researchers, with the dates they were written to. We kept only counts per article: authors in the log, how many had an address on file, and how many replies were logged. Personal pages and ResearchGate profiles were also removed from the article list’s link column.
  • Coder names and dates. Who coded and verified each row, and when. Useful inside a team, not needed to rerun anything.
  • The protocol link and the empty form tab.
  • Empty structure. Unused search-log columns, the empty proportions block, and empty gender columns.
  • Values Excel corrupted. Five rows had a mean age of about 42,900. Those are Excel date serials (42967 is 20 August 2017): whatever was typed in the cell was turned into a date. We blanked them.

Section ten

Rebuild the tables yourself

One script, one input file, eight outputs, and a check against the paper.

Download the OSF archive (osf.io/9fcqm, “Download as zip”) and save it as omission_bias/original/9fcqm-osfstorage-archive.zip. The script pulls the coding sheet out of the zip itself. Then, from the omission_bias/data folder:

source("ob_data_prep.R")
#> ob_01_search_terms.csv          10 rows x  5 cols
#> ...
#> All checks passed: 76 articles -> 13 included, 21 samples, 49 effects.

The script reads each tab with no header, so row and column numbers in R match what you see in Excel, and it selects columns by their Excel letter ("BO" is the t statistic). That makes each line easy to check against the workbook. It ends by testing the counts the paper reports, so if a later version of the workbook changes, the script fails loudly instead of quietly producing different data.

Section eleven

Exercises

  1. Filter the article list to Dropped at full text (no reason recorded). For each of the six, read the abstract and decide which criterion excludes it. Would you have included any of them?
  2. Our criterion column maps free-text reasons to the paper’s criteria. Find two rows in the Excluded table where you would map the reason differently.
  3. The paper counts 63 full-text exclusions by criterion (C1: 2, C2: 31, C3: 7, C4: 8, B1–B2: 15). Try to reproduce that tally from the Coding decisions table. Where do you get stuck?
  4. Pick one included article and one excluded article from the call to authors. Would a reviewer be able to tell from the workbook how each one was found?
  5. In the statistics table, find every row where best_estimate differs from calc_d by more than 0.1. What explains each gap?
