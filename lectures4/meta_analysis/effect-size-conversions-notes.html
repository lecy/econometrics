Section one

Key concepts

The rules for turning a published estimate into a number that can be pooled.

Key concepts

An effect size is an estimate divided by a standard deviation.

Nearly every metric on this page has the same skeleton: take the quantity the study estimated (a mean difference, a regression coefficient, a DiD interaction) and divide it by a standard deviation of the outcome. The choice of which SD is the most consequential coding decision you will make.

Effect sizes from different designs are only poolable if they measure the same contrast.

  • A between-group difference standardized by the outcome SD is one thing.
  • A within-group change standardized by the SD of change scores is another, and it can be much larger for the same raw gain.
  • A coefficient standardized by a residual SD is inflated relative to both.

Every Part 3 estimator produces a coefficient that can be translated.

The dummy coefficient, the ITS level shift, the DiD interaction, the within-unit fixed-effects slope, and the RDD jump are all mean differences in the units of Y. Dividing by an appropriate SD of Y turns each into a standardized mean difference.

Continuous treatments need a reference dose.

A class-size coefficient is “points per student.” To compare it with an experiment that cut classes by a fixed amount, standardize over a stated change in dosage — for example, an 8-student reduction — and fix the sign so that “better” is always positive.

Conversions between metrics carry assumptions.

The standard formulas linking d, r, t, F, and odds ratios mostly assume two groups of equal size, or a logistic latent variable. Know which assumption you are importing.

Every effect size needs a variance.

Inverse-variance weighting is the engine of meta-analysis. An effect size coded without its sampling variance cannot be weighted properly.

Size is judged against the field, not against Cohen.

Cohen’s 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8 benchmarks come from lab psychology. Kraft (2019) proposes that for causal studies of pre-K–12 interventions on achievement, under 0.05 SD is small, 0.05 to under 0.20 is medium, and 0.20 or more is large.

The small-sample correction

Hedges’ correction factor can be written two equivalent ways:

J=1 − 34df − 1 =1 − 34N − 9 , df = N − 2

The first form, with degrees of freedom, is what the R templates use; the second, with total sample size, is what the introductory handout uses. They are the same number: with N = 50, both give 1 − 3/191 = 0.984.

Corrections to the source handout. The Common_Effect_Sizes_and_Conversions.docx table lists r = d/√(d2 + 4) without noting that the 4 assumes equal group sizes, and writes r ≈ β in simple regression. For a standardized slope in simple regression the relationship is exact, r = β; for an unstandardized slope it does not hold at all. The other formulas check out.

Checking the conversions on one example

Use the example from the introductory page: means 80 and 70, SDs 10 and 12, 25 per group, so d = 10/√122 = 0.905.

  • d → t. t = d / √(1/25 + 1/25) = 0.905/0.283 = 3.20.
  • t → d, two ways. The exact 2t/√N = 6.40/7.07 = 0.905 recovers d. The approximation 2t/√df = 6.40/6.93 = 0.924 overstates it by about 2%; the gap shrinks as samples grow.
  • d → r → d. r = 0.905/√(0.820 + 4) = 0.412, and 2(0.412)/√(1 − 0.170) = 0.905. The round trip is exact because the groups are equal.
  • Odds ratio. A logistic study reporting OR = 2 converts to d = ln 2 × 0.551 = 0.38.

The pattern: a coefficient over an SD

Read down the “coefficient” column and every entry is a difference in the units of Y: a treatment–control gap, a level shift, a difference in changes, a jump at a cutoff. That is exactly what Part 3 trained you to interpret. Standardizing is one more division:

d= coefficient (in units of Y)SD of Y

The design determines what the coefficient means — average effect, effect at the cutoff, within-unit effect — and those meanings travel into the effect size. A local RDD effect standardized by the outcome SD is still a local effect.

Which SD? The decision that moves the answer

For the effect sizes to be comparable across designs, divide by an SD that means the same thing in every study: the spread of the outcome across students. That rules out some tempting alternatives.

  • Not the residual SD from a model with controls. Covariates soak up outcome variance, so the residual SD is smaller than the raw SD. Dividing by it inflates the effect size for the same coefficient, and studies with more controls would look more effective. Controls should sharpen the estimate (smaller SE), not change the metric. The spreadsheet’s regression row mentions residual variance; for the effect size itself, use the unadjusted SD of Y and let the covariate adjustment show up in the variance.
  • Not “β/sY, if Y is standardized.” The spreadsheet phrases the regression formula this way, but if Y is already in SD units the coefficient is the effect size and there is nothing to divide by. Divide a raw-units coefficient by the raw SD of Y.
  • Be careful with change-score and within-unit SDs. They strip out stable differences between students or schools and are typically much smaller than the cross-sectional SD. Effects standardized by them are legitimate descriptions of the study, but they are on a different scale from a between-group d. Either convert, or code the metric and treat it as a moderator.

How large can the change-score distortion be? When pre and post SDs are equal, SDchange = SD√[2(1 − r)]. At a pre–post correlation of 0.5 the two SDs coincide. At r = 0.8, typical for achievement tests, SDchange = 0.63 × SD, so the same raw gain produces an effect size 1.58 times larger on the change-score scale (Morris & DeShon, 2002).

What to code for every study

The spreadsheet’s last column is a coding sheet, and it is the practical payoff. Whatever the design, record enough to rebuild the effect size and to use design features as moderators:

  • All designs: design_type, the outcome SD used and where it came from, sample sizes, whether controls were included (controls_included, control_vars, adjusted).
  • Within-subjects: paired = TRUE, the pre–post correlation pre_post_corr, and followup_period.
  • ITS: n_pre, n_post, whether a pre-trend was modeled (pretrend_included), and any autocorrelation adjustment (autocorr_adj).
  • DiD: time_FE, unit_FE, whether parallel trends were tested (parallel_trends_test), n_pre, n_post.
  • Fixed effects: unit_FE, time_FE, and the within variance (within_var).
  • RDD: cutoff_value, bandwidth, functional_form, covariates_included.

Design features like these are at the center of the disagreement in the class size literature. If you do not code them, you cannot test them as moderators later.

The variance that comes with template 1

For a between-group d, the large-sample sampling variance is

Vd= nt + ncntnc + d22(nt + nc)

and for Hedges’ g, Vg = J2Vd (Borenstein et al., 2009). The first term dominates: precision is mostly about sample size. The template plugs the corrected g into the formula and skips the J2, which makes the variance slightly too large — for the 25-per-group example, 0.088 instead of 0.085. The difference is small but grows as samples shrink.

Worked example: a class-size slope

Template 4 is the one the class size case study needs. Observational studies usually report a slope on class size, not a treatment–control difference. Suppose a study reports:

  • β = −0.15 test-score points per additional student in the class,
  • SDY = 12.5 points,
  • and you want the effect of an 8-student reduction, so δ = 8.

Because smaller classes are the “treatment,” the template sets direction = -1 so that a benefit of smaller classes comes out positive:

d=(−1)× (−0.15)(8)12.5 =(−1)× −1.212.5 =0.096 
beta      <- -0.15   # points per additional student
s_y       <- 12.5    # SD of the test score
delta     <- 8       # standardize over an 8-student change
direction <- -1      # smaller classes = better outcome

d <- direction * (beta * delta / s_y)
d
#> [1] 0.096

Three things to notice:

  • The reference dose is a choice, and it scales the answer. Standardize over a 4-student reduction and d = 0.048; over 1 student, 0.012. Every study in a meta-analysis must use the same δ, ideally matched to the reductions actually studied, and the choice assumes the effect is linear over that range.
  • The benchmark changes the verdict. By Cohen’s conventions, 0.096 is below “small.” By Kraft’s benchmarks for causal education studies (0.05 to under 0.20 is medium), the same number is a medium effect. Neither label substitutes for asking what an 8-student reduction costs.
  • Standardization does not fix identification. If the slope comes from a cross-sectional regression where class size is confounded with school resources or student background, d = 0.096 inherits that bias. Code the design so it can be tested as a moderator.

Using the templates with care: three corrections

The templates are a sound starting point, but checking each formula turned up three places where the code and the metric do not quite line up.

  1. Template 2 mixes two metrics. It standardizes by the SD of change scores (dchange) but uses the variance formula 2(1 − r)/n + d2/(2n), which belongs to a change standardized by the raw (pre-test) SD. Pick one pair. For effect sizes that will be pooled with between-group studies, the raw-score version is usually the right one: 
    d   <- direction * (mean_post - mean_pre) / sd_pre   # raw-score SMC
v_d <- 2*(1 - r)/n + d^2/(2*n)
# if you keep sd_change instead, use v_d <- 1/n + d^2/(2*n)
  2. Template 5 divides by the SD of change scores, while the spreadsheet row for DiD divides by the pooled SD of the outcome. Use the pooled outcome SD if the DiD estimate is to sit alongside between-group SMDs; the change-score SD produces a larger number on a different scale.
  3. Template 3 needs the raw SD of Y, not the residual SD from the regression with controls, for the reasons given in Section three.

Templates 1, 4, 6 and 7 are correct as written (apart from the minor J2 point on the variance in templates 1 and 3), and the helper J_correction() is equivalent to the 1 − 3/(4N − 9) version.

Section five

What should be clear in my mind?

What you should be able to do before coding the class size studies.

  1. Every effect size here is an estimate divided by an SD, and the choice of SD determines whether effect sizes from different studies are comparable.
  2. I can convert between d, r, t, F, η2, and odds ratios, and I know which formulas assume equal group sizes.
  3. I can name the coefficient to extract from each Part 3 design: the dummy coefficient, the ITS level shift, the DiD interaction, the within-unit slope, the RDD jump.
  4. Standardize by the raw outcome SD, not a residual SD. Controls should shrink the variance of the estimate, not inflate the effect size.
  5. Change-score effect sizes are on a different scale from between-group effect sizes, and the gap grows with the pre–post correlation.
  6. A continuous treatment needs a stated reference dose and a direction; I can reproduce d = 0.096 for the class-size example.
  7. Every coded effect size needs a sampling variance, or it cannot be weighted.
  8. Design features must be coded so they can be tested as moderators.

Key takeaways

  • Standardizing is the price of comparison. Different tests and units can only be pooled once each result is expressed in SD units.
  • Part 3 estimators are the raw material of meta-analysis. The coefficients you learned to estimate are the numbers a meta-analyst extracts.
  • Which SD you divide by can matter as much as the coefficient.
  • Conversion formulas carry assumptions — equal groups, a logistic latent variable — that should be checked, not assumed.
  • A local or within-unit effect stays local or within-unit after standardization.
  • Benchmarks are field-specific. An effect of 0.10 SD is modest in a lab and respectable for a school intervention.
  • An effect size is only as credible as the design that produced it.
