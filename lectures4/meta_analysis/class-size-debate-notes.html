Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Does Class Size Matter? A Century of Contested Evidence

A reading guide for Unit 24 — the class size literature as a worked example of meta-analysis: why eighty years of studies looked like noise, how a common metric and a regression on effect sizes found the signal, and why the answer still depends on what you count as one piece of evidence.

Section one

Key concepts

The claims the rest of the page defends, each stated in one sentence.

Key concepts

Class size is not randomly assigned, so the raw correlation can point either way.

  • Richer, larger schools have bigger classes and higher scores → bigger classes look good.
  • Struggling students are often put in smaller classes on purpose → smaller classes look bad.
  • Tutoring studies (1 pupil vs. 25) change the whole mode of teaching → smaller classes look spectacular.

This is the omitted variable bias problem from Lab P102, played out across an entire literature.

Vote counting throws away the magnitude, the precision and the contrast.

Tallying “studies for” against “studies against” treats a 0.01 SD effect in a sample of 50 the same as a 0.5 SD effect in a sample of 5,000, and treats a 1-vs-40 comparison the same as a 22-vs-28 one.

Meta-analysis needs a common metric before it can do anything else.

ΔS−L= XS − XLσ

The small-class mean minus the large-class mean, in within-class standard deviations. Any test, any subject, any decade.

Effect sizes can be the outcome of a regression.

Glass and Smith regressed 725 effect sizes on the class sizes being compared. The moderators (S, S2, L − S, study quality, grade level) are the regressors. The payoff was a curve, not a yes/no.

The unit of analysis is a modeling choice with real consequences.

  • One estimate, one vote (Hanushek) → no systematic effect.
  • One study, one vote (Krueger) → a positive effect.
  • One effect size vs. one study vs. one state (Shin & Chung) → d = 0.20, 0.17, 0.08.

A single dominant study is a dependence problem, not just a strength.

Tennessee’s STAR experiment supplies 78 of the 120 effect sizes in Shin and Chung (2009). The best evidence is also the most repeated evidence, and the pooled standard error pretends those 78 are independent.

The consensus is conditional: small classes help most in K–3, and most for disadvantaged students.

A single pooled slope averages over that heterogeneity. The modeling exercise asks you to recover it with a grade × class-size interaction.

Section two

A policy question that is expensive to get wrong

Why class size is worth a century of argument.

Class size reduction (CSR) is one of the most popular education reforms and one of the most expensive. Every student removed from a classroom has to go somewhere, which means more teachers, more classrooms and sometimes more buildings. Shin and Chung (2009) open their meta-analysis with cost estimates from Brewer, Krop, Gill and Reichardt (1999): a nationwide CSR program in the United States would cost somewhere between about $2 billion and over $11 billion per year in operating costs, depending on the policy design — before construction, and before the salary pressure that comes from hiring that many teachers at once.

The stakes run in both directions:

  • If class size matters and we ignore it, we leave learning on the table — and the literature suggests the losses fall hardest on disadvantaged and minority students.
  • If it doesn’t, or matters only a little, the same billions could buy more through teacher quality, curriculum or targeted interventions.

This is the setting you met in Lab P102 and in the omitted variable bias worked example: a program whose effect is small relative to the noise, estimated from data where the program was not assigned at random. This unit steps back from a single regression to the whole literature and asks: what does the body of evidence say, and how do you combine it?

Why this is a meta-analysis case study. Class size has almost every problem a research synthesis can have: hundreds of studies, no common outcome scale, wildly different “treatments,” confounding in both directions, one dominant experiment, and a public fight between two economists over how to count the evidence. Working through it teaches the method better than a clean example would.

Section three

Why the literature looked like noise

Four problems that made eighty years of research unreadable.

Empirical work on class size goes back at least to Rice (1902), which Glass and Smith (1979) identify as the first empirical study of educational processes and achievement. By the late 1970s the topic was, in Glass and Smith’s phrase, one where review after review had “dissolved into cynical despair or epistemological confusion,” and most instructional researchers treated it as a dead issue. They blamed the reviews rather than the studies.

1. Reviewers counted votes

The standard narrative review tallied studies. Blake (1954), as summarized by Shin and Chung, read 85 studies and found 35 favoring small classes, 18 favoring large classes and 32 inconclusive. That looks like weak evidence. But vote counting ignores how big each effect was, how precise it was, and how good the design was. A literature full of small, underpowered studies of a real but modest effect will produce exactly this kind of split — Glass and Smith estimated that in the typical 15-to-40 range, the odds of any one comparison even favoring the smaller class might be as low as 55 to 45. Searches were also haphazard and selective: dissertations and unpublished reports were usually skipped.

2. There was no common metric

Studies used different tests, different subjects, grades from kindergarten to college, and outcomes ranging from standardized test scores to promotion rates. Without a common scale there is nothing to average. The early reviewers who tried quantitative summaries used crude class-size categories and, Glass and Smith complained, took statistical significance far too seriously.

3. The “treatments” were not the same treatment

“Smaller versus larger” covered everything from 1-on-1 tutoring compared with a class of 25, to college lectures of 20 versus 138, to 22 versus 28 within a single district. A 1-vs-40 contrast and a 22-vs-28 contrast are different interventions. Counting them as equivalent evidence guarantees confusion, because the effect of removing one student almost certainly depends on where you start.

4. Confounding pushed in both directions

This is the part you already know from the OVB lectures. Class size is chosen by districts, principals and parents, and the things that drive those choices also drive achievement. The confounders do not all lean the same way, which is exactly why the raw literature looked contradictory.

DirectionMechanismWhat it does to the naive estimate
Makes large classes look better SES and enrollment. Higher-SES schools and districts tend to be larger and have more students per class; their students score higher for reasons unrelated to class size. Positive correlation between class size and scores.
Teacher seniority. Experienced teachers may be assigned, or may choose, the larger sections. Teacher quality rides along with larger classes.
Compensatory assignment. Schools put weaker or more disruptive students in smaller classes (Krueger cites Lazear’s model), and compensatory funding sends more staff to low-achieving schools. Small classes are full of students who would score low anyway — reverse causation.
Makes small classes look better Tutoring modality. Comparing 1 pupil with 25 captures a change in how teaching is done, not just head count. Very large effects that don’t generalize to 22 vs. 28.
Novelty. Short experiments may pick up Hawthorne or novelty effects. Early gains that fade.
Missing pretests. Without a baseline, any pre-existing difference between the small and large classes is attributed to class size. Bias of unknown sign — whichever way the groups already differed.

In OVB terms: the bias is b2 × a1 for each omitted variable, and different omitted variables carry different signs. A literature that mixes contexts mixes the signs of the bias.

The aggregate-trend argument

Hanushek (2002) adds a historical version of the same problem. U.S. pupil–teacher ratios fell by about a third between 1960 and 1995 (from 25.8 to 17.3), a bigger reduction than most proposals on the table, and real spending per pupil roughly tripled. Yet SAT scores fell over much of the period and NAEP math and reading scores for 17-year-olds were nearly flat. If class size mattered much, he argues, we should see it in the national data.

Krueger’s reply (Chapter 3 of the same volume) is a confounding argument:

  • The SAT is voluntary. As a larger share of students took it, the marginal test-taker was weaker and the average fell — a change in who is measured, not in what they learned.
  • Within the NAEP data, the correlation between scores and the pupil–teacher ratio is negative, with a slope close to what STAR would predict.
  • It is odd to rest the case on aggregate time-series data while criticizing aggregate cross-sectional estimates as low quality.

The lesson is not who won. It is that the same historical data support opposite conclusions depending on what you assume the omitted variables are doing.

Section four

Glass and Smith (1979): the first meta-analysis of class size

A common metric, a regression on effect sizes, and coded study characteristics.

Gene Glass had coined “meta-analysis” a few years earlier and applied it to psychotherapy (Smith & Glass, 1977). The class size paper made three moves that are now the standard toolkit.

Move one: a standardized effect size

Every comparison of a smaller class (size S) with a larger class (size L) was converted to

ΔS−L= XS − XLσ

where σ is the within-class standard deviation. A positive Δ favors the smaller class. When studies reported only t or F statistics, correlations, or pass/fail proportions, these were converted to Δ (probits for proportions). One study could supply many Δs: one for each pair of class sizes, each test, each age group.

Move two: search widely and code everything

They read about 300 documents, including dissertations and unpublished reports, and kept 77 studies yielding 725 effect sizes, based on nearly 900,000 pupils, spanning 70 years of research in more than a dozen countries. Each comparison was coded for year, source (journal, book, thesis, unpublished), subject, hours of instruction, pupil IQ, age, type of test, and — crucially — how pupils were assigned: random (15% of comparisons), matched (32%), repeated measures (3%) or uncontrolled (50%).

Move three: regress the effect sizes on the contrast

The raw average was unimpressive: mean Δ = 0.088 (median 0.05), and only 60% of the 725 comparisons favored the smaller class. Glass and Smith’s point was that this average is meaningless because it ignores which class sizes were compared. So they modeled it:

ΔS−L= β0+ β1S+ β2S2+ β3(L − S)+ ε

The S2 term lets the benefit of a one-student reduction depend on the starting size; L − S captures how big the contrast is. Fit by OLS to all 725 points (multiple R = .426):

Δ̂= .571 − .0386S + .00059S2 + .00082(L − S)

The relationship is strongly nonlinear. Going from 40 to 30 buys essentially nothing; going from 20 to 10, or 10 to 1, buys a lot. Tutoring (1 vs. 40) is worth more than half a standard deviation; 20 vs. 40 is worth about five hundredths.

What moderated the effect

Rerunning the regression on subsets of the data is a moderator analysis. Most splits (source, subject, duration, pupil IQ, type of test) produced nearly identical curves. Three did not:

  • Study quality. The 108 comparisons with random assignment produced a much steeper curve than the 334 “uncontrolled” ones (mean Δ .401 vs. .051; the regression fit, R = .621 vs. .187, was also far tighter). The well-controlled studies also compared smaller classes on average, so the gap is partly about the contrasts studied — but the regressions condition on S and L and the gap remains. Glass and Smith concluded the well-controlled curve was the best representation of the relationship. Dropping the tutoring comparisons (S = 1) made that curve steeper, not flatter.
  • Era. No relationship in studies before 1940; a strong one in post-1960 studies, which also had much better designs and measures.
  • Level of schooling. The curve was somewhat steeper for secondary pupils (age 12+) than for elementary pupils (1 vs. 40: .749 vs. .490). This is the opposite of the later consensus — see Section seven.

Their bottom line: between classes of 40 and 1 lie more than 30 percentile ranks of achievement, and moving from 20 to 10 can be worth more than 10 percentile ranks. “Other things equal, more is learned in smaller classes.”

Notice what Glass and Smith did not do. They reported no standard errors or significance tests, on purpose. Many comparisons came from the same study, there was “no sensible way to reduce each study to one observation,” and the studies were not a sample from any population. They named the dependence problem in 1979. It is the problem the next thirty years of the debate are about.

The paper was also criticized. Hedges and Stock (1983) reanalyzed the data with improved methods and questioned the conclusions, and Educational Research Service (1980) argued that only 14 of the studies were well controlled and that 73% of the data behind the conclusions came from just four of those 14 (both as summarized by Shin & Chung, 2009). A few studies supplying most of the effect sizes is the same concentration problem that reappears with STAR.

Section five

Hanushek vs. Krueger: what counts as one piece of evidence?

The same 277 estimates, two weighting schemes, two conclusions.

The Class Size Debate (Mishel & Rothstein, 2002) puts both sides in one volume: Krueger’s critique (Chapter 1), Hanushek’s response (Chapter 2), Krueger’s reply (Chapter 3), and a policy chapter by Jennifer King Rice (Chapter 4). The dispute is over Hanushek’s (1997) tabulation of 277 estimates of the class size effect drawn from 59 published studies of education production functions.

Hanushek: one estimate, one vote

Hanushek counts every regression estimate as a separate result. Of the 277, 14% were positive and significant (smaller classes help), 14% were negative and significant, and the insignificant ones split roughly evenly, with a fifth not even reporting a sign. Conclusion: no consistent relationship, so a general policy of CSR is unlikely to raise achievement.

Krueger: one study, one vote

Krueger points out that the estimates are very unevenly distributed across studies. Nine of the 59 studies (15%) contributed 44% of the estimates; 17 studies contributed one estimate each. Two papers alone supplied 24 estimates apiece by splitting the same data by grade (4) × race (3) × subject (2). Counting each estimate equally gives those papers 24 times the weight of a study that reported one pooled estimate from the full sample — and split-sample estimates are less precise and so more likely to come out insignificant or wrong-signed. Reweighting so each study counts once changes the picture:

Same studies, same coding, different unit of analysis — and the ratio of positive to negative findings goes from a coin flip to two to one.

Hanushek’s reply

Hanushek answers that the publications with many estimates do not actually have systematically smaller samples (the correlation is about 0.03), and that each estimate is a distinct empirical exercise. His quality argument runs the other way: the most credible estimates use data within a single state, while estimates from state-level aggregate data — which are the most vulnerable to omitted state policy differences — are the ones most likely to be positive (64% positive and significant, from only 11 estimates). Equal weighting by study, he argues, ends up leaning on low-quality aggregate work.

The general lesson. A research synthesis has to decide what an independent observation is. Weighting by estimate rewards authors who slice their data thin. Weighting by study treats a small study and a large one as equals. Formal meta-analysis weights by precision (inverse variance) — which, as Krueger notes, is why Hedges, Laine and Greenwald (1994) reached a different conclusion than the vote count — but it still cannot fix misspecified primary studies. As Krueger puts it, research is not democratic: one good study can be worth more than the rest of the literature.

Section six

Tennessee STAR and the Shin & Chung (2009) meta-analysis

The one good experiment, and what happens when it dominates the pool.

Project STAR

Beginning in 1985, Tennessee paid volunteering schools to randomly assign kindergarten students and teachers to small classes (13–17), regular classes (22–25), or regular classes with a teacher’s aide, and followed them through third grade. The initial sample was 6,324 kindergartners in 79 schools. Studies of STAR (Finn & Achilles, 1990; Krueger, 1999) find that cutting class size from about 22 to 15 raised math and reading scores by about 0.2 standard deviations, and Finn and Achilles (1999) report effects for minority students roughly double those for white students. Mosteller (1995) called it one of the most important educational investigations ever carried out.

Random assignment removes the confounding problems of Section three in principle. Hanushek (2002) lists the ways practice fell short: no pretests to check the randomization; sizable attrition and replacement each year (48% of the kindergarten cohort stayed all four years); schools self-selected and oversampled urban and minority schools; small classes beat regular classes in only 40 of the 79 schools; and the small-class advantage appeared almost entirely in the first year rather than growing. None of this makes STAR uninformative. It means that one experiment, however good, is one draw.

Shin and Chung (2009): a random-effects meta-analysis

Shin and Chung searched ERIC, ISI, JSTOR, PsycINFO and UMI for U.S. K–12 studies from 1989 to 2008 comparing small classes with regular or large ones on standardized tests. Of 129 studies found, 17 studies yielding 120 effect sizes met their criteria. The effect size is the standardized mean difference with a pooled standard deviation:

d= Xsmall − XlargeSpooled

The effect sizes were strongly heterogeneous (Q = 719.4, df = 119), so they used a random-effects model, which assumes each study estimates its own true effect drawn from a distribution, rather than one common effect. The headline:

Overall d = 0.20 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.22, k = 120). Students in small classes score about a fifth of a standard deviation higher.

Moderators were examined with fixed-effects categorical analyses (subgroup means):

  • Publication status: published studies d = 0.21 (k = 76) vs. unpublished 0.13 (k = 44). The authors read this as evidence of publication bias.
  • Design: random assignment 0.20 (k = 90) vs. non-random 0.11 (k = 21) — like Glass and Smith, the better designs show larger effects.
  • School level: elementary 0.20 (k = 114) vs. secondary −0.05 (k = 6, CI −0.35 to 0.25). The secondary estimate rests on six effect sizes from one 10th-grade study using SAT/ACT scores.
  • Grade: K 0.17, 1st 0.24, 2nd 0.20, 3rd 0.16, 4th 0.13, 5th 0.20, 10th −0.05. A meta-regression on grade gives an intercept of .22 and a slope of −.01 per grade (p = .08): the effect shrinks as grade rises.
  • Subject: reading 0.19, math 0.20, social science 0.20; writing, science, ACT and SAT rest on one to three effect sizes each and are inconclusive.
  • State: Tennessee 0.22, Wisconsin 0.20, Indiana 0.14, North Carolina 0.26, and negative in South Carolina, Georgia and West Virginia.

The dependence problem

Here is the catch the authors themselves flag. There are two layers of dependence:

  • Within studies: about seven effect sizes per study, often the same students tested in several subjects.
  • Between studies: 8 of the 17 studies analyze STAR, and STAR supplies 78 of the 120 effect sizes.

Treating 120 effect sizes as 120 independent observations overstates precision and lets one experiment set the answer. Following Cooper’s “shifting unit of analysis,” Shin and Chung re-estimate the overall effect with different units:

With the state as the unit, Tennessee counts once instead of 78 times, and the estimate falls from 0.20 to 0.08 while the standard error almost triples. Shin and Chung still prefer the effect-size unit, on the grounds that STAR is the best-controlled evidence and its studies are not identical. That is a defensible call — and it is exactly the Hanushek–Krueger argument in another form. The answer depends on what you count as one piece of evidence.

Read the tables, not just the text. The paper is not internally consistent in a few places: the abstract says 8 published and 9 unpublished studies while the methods say 6 and 11; the text gives the unpublished mean as .15 but Table 4 reports .13; and one passage refers to 121 effect sizes. This page uses the table values. Small inconsistencies like these are common, and they are one reason meta-analysts code from tables and re-derive numbers where they can.

Why the elementary/secondary results disagree

This is the most instructive row. Glass and Smith’s secondary comparisons covered a much wider range of contrasts (average L − S of 26 vs. 14 in elementary) and included college lecture studies (the and included college lecture studies and tutoring.ldquo;secondary and included college lecture studies and tutoring.rdquo; group was everyone aged 12 and over). Shin and Chung’s literature is dominated by statewide K–3 policy experiments, so their secondary estimate comes from one study. Neither meta-analysis can separate “grade level” from “what kind of study happened to be done at that grade level.” A moderator in a meta-analysis is an observational variable across studies, and it can be confounded just like a regressor in a primary study.

Section eight

The post-meta-analysis consensus, and its limits

What most researchers now accept, what remains disputed, and what it means for policy.

What is broadly accepted

  • Smaller classes do raise achievement, with effects concentrated in the early grades (K–3) and larger for disadvantaged and minority students.
  • The effect is nonlinear. Cuts at the low end of the range matter more than cuts from 40 to 30. Groups under about 10 blend into the tutoring literature.
  • The magnitude is modest. Around 0.2 SD for large reductions (about seven or eight students) in the early grades — meaningful, not transformative.
  • Evidence for later grades is thin. Very few rigorous secondary-school studies exist.

Is 0.2 SD worth the money?

Krueger (2002) reframes the question. Testing whether the effect is zero is the wrong null for a policymaker; the right question is how large an effect would justify the cost. Assuming a 1 SD gain in K–3 scores raises later earnings by 8%, a 4% discount rate and 1% annual productivity growth, the critical effect size for cutting classes from 22 to 15 is about 0.10 SD (about 0.010 SD for a one-student cut). STAR’s estimates exceed it. Hanushek (2002) calls the chain of assumptions “heroic” and argues the right comparison is not CSR versus nothing, but CSR versus other uses of the money.

What remains disputed

  • How much rests on STAR. The pooled estimate drops from 0.20 to 0.08 when Tennessee counts once.
  • Teacher quality. Hanushek argues teacher quality matters far more than class size, and that CSR has an opportunity cost: hiring many new teachers at once draws from the weaker end of the pool.
  • Whether a pilot scales. STAR’s schools volunteered and had the classrooms and teachers to spare. A statewide mandate does not.

California, 1996–97: the opportunity cost made visible

California adopted CSR in 1996–97, paying schools to cap K–3 classes at 20 (down from some of the largest classes in the country). Mishel and Rothstein’s introduction describes what followed: the sudden demand for teachers led districts to hire many teachers without training or credentials, while experienced teachers in low-income and minority schools moved to the new openings in suburban districts. Scores rose afterward, but California changed other policies at the same time, so the CSR effect is hard to isolate. Jepsen and Rivkin (2009) use year-to-year variation in class size to separate the two channels: smaller classes raised math and reading achievement, but the rise in inexperienced, not fully certified teachers dampened the gains — most in schools with many disadvantaged, minority students.

Policy implications

  • CSR is most defensible in K–3, and most of all for disadvantaged and minority students.
  • Blanket CSR across all grades is hard to justify given how little evidence supports effects in secondary school.
  • CSR pays off only if the new teachers are good. The constraint binds hardest in the lowest-SES districts — the ones with the most to gain.
  • Scale and speed matter: a policy that works as a pilot can undermine itself when implemented all at once.

Section nine

From the meta-analysis to the modeling exercise

A synthetic panel built so you can watch each problem in the literature happen in your own regressions.

The unit’s modeling exercise uses a synthetic classroom-year panel (1,248 classroom-year observations across 8 schools) whose data-generating process was built to reproduce the main features of the class size literature. Because the true parameters are known, you can check which specifications recover them and which don’t — something no real dataset allows. (The dataset itself is not yet posted on the course site; what follows describes how it was built.)

How the confounding is built in

  • free_lunch (an SES proxy) is negatively correlated with mother_edu and with pretest scores, and related to class size in some schools — the school-level SES gradient.
  • class_size is partly driven by a school size factor that is itself correlated with school SES. Lower-SES schools have smaller enrollments and so smaller sections.
  • teacher_exp is positively correlated with holding a graduate degree, so dropping either one biases the other’s coefficient.
  • Pretest scores absorb much of the SES signal: once pretest is in the model, the free_lunch and mother_edu coefficients shrink.
  • The class size effect is negative (smaller is better) and declines with grade, largest in K–2 and smallest in grades 9–12, mirroring Shin and Chung’s grade gradient.

The reversal problem: naive OLS gets the sign wrong

Regress test_score on class_size alone and the slope is typically positive: bigger classes appear to produce higher scores. That is not a bug in the simulation. It is the pattern that confused narrative reviewers for decades. Low-SES schools have smaller sections and lower scores for reasons that have nothing to do with class size, and the SES gradient swamps the true negative effect. In OVB terms, the omitted SES variable has a negative effect on scores and a negative correlation with class size, so the bias is positive and large enough to flip the sign.

The model sequence walks the estimate back toward the truth:

(The source lists these five steps of the seven-model sequence; the intermediate models add controls in the same spirit.)

The unit-of-analysis choice, again

The Hanushek–Krueger dispute has a direct analogue in the panel:

  • Pool all 1,248 classroom-years in one OLS model and every observation gets equal weight — the analogue of one estimate, one vote.
  • Run a separate regression in each of the 8 schools and average, and each “site” gets equal weight — the analogue of one study, one vote.
  • Fixed effects for teacher, school and year use only within-unit variation — closest in spirit to a controlled experiment.

These produce different point estimates. None is automatically correct; each embeds an assumption about which observations are independent.

Grade heterogeneity: fixed effects vs. interactions

Both meta-analyses found that the effect depends on grade, even if they disagreed on the direction. The synthetic data build in a gradient, and there are two ways to model it:

The pooled slope is not wrong. It is an average, and the average hides the policy-relevant fact that the effect is large for one part of the population and small for another. Allocating CSR money on the pooled estimate would overspend in high school and underspend in kindergarten. This is the same move Glass and Smith made when they refused to report a single average Δ: the average is a function of which cases happen to be in the pool.

Interpreting the magnitude

The synthetic test scores are centered at 50 with a standard deviation of about 12.5 points. Suppose the class size coefficient is −0.15:

  • Each additional student costs about 0.15 points.
  • Going from a class of 15 to a class of 25 (10 more students) costs about 10 × 0.15 = 1.5 points.
  • In standard-deviation units:
1.512.5=0.12 SD

How does that compare with the literature? It sits right at Glass and Smith’s regression prediction for 15 vs. 25 (Δ = .133). It is smaller per student than STAR, which found about 0.2 SD for a reduction of seven or eight students, or roughly 0.03 SD per student against 0.012 here. The synthetic data are calibrated to be plausible, not to reproduce any single study. And the pooled coefficient averages over grades, so the K–2 effect in the interaction model will be closer to the experimental benchmarks.

Section ten

Questions to work through

For the modeling exercise. Write down your answer before you run the model.

  1. The naive regression of scores on class size alone gives a positive coefficient. Before running anything, sketch which variables you think drive this reversal. Which confounders would you control for first, and in what order? For each one, predict the sign of the bias it creates.
  2. Hanushek notes that pupil–teacher ratios fell for 35 years with no visible gain in aggregate scores. What omitted variables or confounders would have to be present to reconcile that history with a genuine positive effect of smaller classes? Which of Krueger’s objections is an omitted variable argument, and which is a measurement argument?
  3. Adding grade fixed effects changes the class_size coefficient. Is the fixed-effects model identifying a different quantity from the pooled model? What must hold for each to be unbiased?
  4. Compare the pooled grade-FE slope with the average of the grade-specific interaction slopes. When would you expect them to differ? Under what weighting scheme would they be equal? (Hint: think about which grades have more observations, and more variation in class size.)
  5. Shin and Chung found published studies average d = 0.21 and unpublished studies 0.13. What mechanisms could produce that gap besides publication bias in the file-drawer sense? (Consider that most published effect sizes come from STAR.) What does the gap imply about the true population effect?
  6. The teacher fixed-effects model identifies the effect from within-teacher changes in class size across years. What is the identifying assumption? What threat to it exists in this panel — for example, if teachers with a hard year are given a smaller class the next — and does it matter for interpretation?
  7. Shin and Chung’s estimate falls from 0.20 to 0.08 when the state is the unit of analysis. Make the strongest case for each choice. Which would you report to a state legislature considering CSR, and why?

Section eleven

What should be clear in my mind?

If you can explain each of these without notes, you have the unit.

  1. Why the class size literature looked contradictory for eighty years: vote counting, no common metric, incomparable contrasts, and confounders pushing in both directions.
  2. What an effect size is and why meta-analysis needs one: the standardized mean difference puts every study on the same scale.
  3. How Glass and Smith used regression on effect sizes, and why modeling S, S2 and L − S revealed a nonlinear relationship that a simple average hid.
  4. Why the unit of analysis matters: one estimate, one study, or one state can give different answers from identical data.
  5. What random effects means and why Shin and Chung used it (significant heterogeneity across effect sizes).
  6. Why STAR is both the best evidence and a problem for the pooled estimate: 78 of 120 effect sizes, 0.20 vs. 0.08.
  7. Why naive OLS on class size can have the wrong sign, and which controls or fixed effects fix it.
  8. The difference between a pooled slope with grade fixed effects and a grade × class size interaction, and why the difference matters for policy.

Key takeaways

  • A literature can look like noise when it is really a modest effect studied with small, confounded samples. Vote counting cannot tell the difference; meta-analysis can.
  • The first requirement of synthesis is a common metric — and the second is to keep track of what was compared, because a 1-vs-40 effect and a 22-vs-28 effect are not the same quantity.
  • Moderators are regressors. Meta-analysis is regression with effect sizes as the outcome, and it inherits regression’s problems, including confounded moderators.
  • Better-designed studies show larger class size effects in both meta-analyses. The confounding in the observational literature mostly hid the effect.
  • Smaller classes help most in K–3 and for disadvantaged students, by about 0.2 SD for a reduction of seven or eight students.
  • How you count evidence is a substantive choice. Hanushek’s and Krueger’s weights, and Shin and Chung’s three units of analysis, lead to different conclusions from the same data.
  • Policy scale changes the treatment. California showed that hiring a large number of teachers at once can erode teacher quality and offset the benefit of smaller classes.
  • A pooled estimate is a weighted average. Whether it is the right number depends on whether the decision you face is pooled too.
