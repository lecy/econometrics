Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Replicating Shin and Chung: Nye et al. (1992)

This is the fifth replication case study. The Lasting Benefits Study followed STAR pupils after they went back to regular classes. This report covers Grade 5, two years after the experiment ended. It prints means and effect sizes but no standard deviations. It is also the one study in Shin and Chung’s sample whose subgroup estimate can be reproduced exactly, which shows how they coded it. Case studies 1, 2, 3, and 4 come first. The R code is in replication/nye_1992.R.

Section one

Key concepts

What this case study adds, stated as claims.

Key concepts

A follow-up study answers a different question.

Every Grade 5 pupil here was back in a regular-size class. The effects measure whether a K–3 benefit persisted, not what small classes do in Grade 5. A meta-analysis that codes this study as “grade 5” and fits a trend across grades mixes the two questions.

You can sometimes reverse-engineer a meta-analysis.

Shin and Chung publish no list of effect sizes. But this is their only Grade 5 study, so their Grade 5 row must come from it. Only one subset of this report’s effects reproduces their k, mean, SE, and Q. That reveals which outcome they dropped and that they treated seven correlated effects as independent.

Balanced at randomization is not the same as balanced at follow-up.

After four years of moves, new entrants, and attrition, 31.5% of former small-class pupils were minority, against 37.9% of regular-class pupils. Part of the raw gap is composition.

A missing SD can be recovered, within a range.

difference ÷ rounded effect size → SD ± the rounding error

Section four

Step one: check the tables

The differences reproduce. The SDs can be recovered. One row of means is a copy error.

The differences reproduce

Subtracting the Appendix B means reproduces all sixteen differences in Table 7 to the second decimal: for example, 722.95 − 712.42 = 10.53 for Total Reading.

Recovering the missing SDs

Since d = difference / SD, the SD is the difference divided by d. The effect sizes are rounded to two decimals, so each SD is really a range:

SDreading = 10.530.22 =47.9    (46.8 to 49.0 for d between .215 and .225)

The NRT scales give SDs of about 45–59 points, and the domain counts about 2.4–2.5. The aide column, which uses the same SD, agrees to rounding: for Science, −2.66 / 52.9 = −0.050, as printed.

A copied row in Appendix B

Appendix B lists the minority Total Math means as 706.62, 700.65, and 698.41. Those are exactly the minority Science means. Table 6 gives 724.45, 714.41, and 717.49. The overall mean settles it. With 68.5% white pupils in small classes, Table 6 gives 0.685(735.00) + 0.315(724.45) = 731.68, matching the printed 731.69. Appendix B gives 726.06. Code minority Total Math from Table 6.

Cross-check with case study 2. The Grade 5 column of Finn and Achilles (1999, Table 2) repeats these eight effect sizes exactly. Their table note, though, says Grades 5–7 “used common within-cell standard deviations,” while this report says it used the regular-class SD. Both can’t be true for the same numbers. Without the SDs there is no way to tell which is right, so record both in sd_type. The case 2 coding now flags its Grade 5 rows as duplicates of this study.

Section five

Reproducing Shin and Chung’s Grade 5 row

For once, the meta-analysis can be checked number for number.

Shin and Chung’s Table 2 lists this report as “Nye, et al., 1992, Report, random = 1, STAR, R, M, SS, published = 0, grade 5.” It is their only Grade 5 study. So their Table 7 row for Grade 5 (k = 7, d = .20, SE = .0137, Q = 7.3) must be built from these eight effects, minus one.

Give each effect the usual variance with the reported group sizes (1,578 small, 1,467 regular), then pool every possible set of seven with a fixed-effect model (Step 6 of the R script):

Shin & Chung, Table 77.20.01377.3

Only one subset matches on all four numbers: the six NRT scales plus math domains, with language arts domains left out. Two things follow:

  • They dropped one outcome and kept its twin. The two domain-count measures are built the same way. Keeping math and dropping language arts (the largest effect) has no obvious rationale, and none is reported.
  • They treated seven effects on the same pupils as independent. An SE of .0137 is 0.036 / √7: what you get from seven independent samples of 3,045 pupils. These are seven tests taken by the same 3,045 pupils. With a modest correlation of .7 between tests, the SE of their average is about 0.031, and about 0.054 once classroom clustering is allowed for. Their SE is about a quarter of what the data can support.

Section six

The decision points

Six choices. The first changes how the whole grade trend in Shin and Chung should be read.

1. Persistence is not a Grade 5 treatment effect

ProblemShin and Chung code this study as grade = 5 and fit a meta-regression on grade (slope −0.01 per grade). But nobody here was in a small class in Grade 5. The Grade 5 effect is a K–3 effect measured two years later.

ChoiceCode grade = 5 and timing = "2 years after treatment ended". Leave follow-up rows out of any analysis of grade as a moderator of the treatment effect.

WhyA grade trend that mixes during-treatment and after-treatment effects can’t say whether small classes work less well in later grades or whether early effects fade.

2. What “small class” means here

ProblemPupils are grouped by Grade 3 class type. At most 570 of the 1,578 “small” pupils (36%) could have had all four years. Many joined STAR in Grade 1, 2, or 3.

ChoiceRecord exposure = "small class in grade 3; 1–4 years". Treat the effect as the effect of ending K–3 in a small class, averaged over doses.

3. Which comparison group?

ChoiceSmall vs. regular, as reported.

WhyThis matches the rule from case study 1 (aide classes are a different treatment). It is also the report’s own contrast, because pupils were swapped between the regular and aide arms after kindergarten (p. 6). The aide arm sits close to regular on every measure (aide minus regular from −0.05 to +0.07).

4. Racial composition at follow-up

ProblemMinority share is 31.5% among former small-class pupils and 37.9% among regular-class pupils (Table 1). Minority pupils score 15 to 32 points lower on every scale (Table 6).

ChoiceKeep the reported d as primary and add a race-standardized version (d_race_std). Take the small vs. regular difference within each race, then weight both by the same race shares (34.6% minority overall).

EffectA sixth to a quarter of each raw gap is composition. The imbalance is not evidence of broken randomization. It probably comes from who stayed in STAR schools and who joined them. But a follow-up sample is not a randomized sample.

5. Two kinds of outcome

ProblemThe domain-count measures give larger effects (.34, .28) than the scale scores (.17–.22). They count objectives passed at a 75% cutoff, which magnifies differences near the cutoff.

ChoiceTotal Reading and Total Math are primary. Code the other six with test_type so they can be included or excluded by rule. Shin and Chung’s rule seems to have been “keep one domain count, drop the other.”

6. Variance and clustering

ChoiceNaive variance from the reported group sizes (SE about 0.036 per effect), with the case study 1 design effect (2.93) stored as an upper bound (SE about 0.062).

WhyBy Grade 5, pupils from one STAR class were spread across new classes and schools, so clustering by the original treatment class is weaker than it was in Grade 1. It is not zero, since pupils shared a teacher for the treatment years. The truth lies between the two SEs.

Section eight

Exercises

Each changes one step in nye_1992.R.

  1. Redo Shin and Chung’s Grade 5 row honestly. Pool the same seven effects with a covariance matrix assuming r = .7 between tests (as in case study 2, Step 7). How wide is the 95% CI now, compared with their .17 to .24?
  2. The grade trend without follow-ups. Using Shin and Chung’s Table 7, refit the grade meta-regression without Grade 5. Does the slope change sign or size? What does that say about mixing persistence with treatment effects?
  3. Rounding. Take the low and high ends of each recovered SD. How far can the race-standardized effects move? Is the composition adjustment larger or smaller than the rounding uncertainty?
  4. Fade-out. Finn and Achilles (1999, Table 2) give Grade 4 effects of 0.11–0.16 and Grade 5 effects of 0.17–0.34. The Grade 4 sample lost 17 schools, and its minority share fell from 34% to 20% (p. 3). Could the rise from Grade 4 to Grade 5 be a sampling artifact? What would you need to check?
  5. Which domain count? Write a coding rule that decides, before seeing any effect sizes, whether domain-count outcomes are included. Apply it to this study and to the BSF pass rates in case study 1.
