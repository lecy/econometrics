Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Replicating Shin and Chung: Finn and Achilles (1990)

This is the first of the replication case studies. Shin and Chung (2009) list Finn and Achilles (1990) as one of their 17 studies, but they don't say which numbers they took from it. This guide rebuilds that coding step by step. It shows where each statistic sits in the article and which decisions you have to make along the way. Read Coding Class Size Studies first. The R code is in replication/finn_achilles_1990.R.

Section one

Key concepts

What this replication is meant to teach, stated as claims.

Key concepts

Reproduce the authors’ numbers before computing your own.

If you can get back the effect sizes printed in the paper, you know what each table means, which SD was used, and how the contrast was defined. If you can’t, you have misread something. Work that out before going further.

Most of the work is finding the right standard deviation.

The means are in one table. The SD you need is in a different table, in a row explained only by a footnote. A second SD, computed across class means, is never printed at all. Using the wrong one nearly triples the effect size.

Papers rarely report everything a meta-analyst needs.

  • This paper gives no student counts per arm, no SD for small classes, and no test statistics, only p-value thresholds.
  • You will impute some numbers. That is normal. Leaving the imputation undocumented is the problem.

Every decision changes the number, so write every decision down.

control group → standardizer → sample sizes → dichotomous outcomes → clustering → multiple outcomes → what to leave out

For SAT reading, the same article supports an effect size anywhere from 0.23 to 0.64, depending on these choices.

Section four

Step one: reproduce the authors’ effect sizes

Confirm you’ve read the tables correctly before making any choices of your own.

Table 5 reports a small-class effect of .23 for SAT reading, “student SD.” Following the Table 6 footnote, the contrast is small minus the average of the other two arms, divided by the regular-class student SD:

Δ= 521.8 − (505.1 + 512.7)/255.48 = 12.955.48 =0.233

The same calculation for all five outcomes (Step 2 of the R script):

Word study skills11.600.225.22
SAT reading12.900.233.23
BSF reading10.050.207.21
SAT math11.200.269.27
BSF math5.250.135.13

All five match to rounding. This confirms two things you couldn’t be sure of from the text:

  1. The unlabeled SD row in Table 3 is the standardizer.
  2. For BSF, the effect size is a percentage-point difference divided by the SD of a 0/1 score. That holds even though the significance tests used log-odds. The Table 5 footnote mentions both, and it’s easy to misread.

Try the race subgroups too. Applying the same contrast to Table 4 reproduces Table 6. The exception is minority SAT math: the rounded means give .30, and Table 6 prints .31. Table 6 lists the mean difference as 11.6, but the rounded means give 11.55. Discrepancies of one in the last digit usually come from rounding like this. They aren’t coding errors, but note them.

Section five

The decision points

Seven choices between the printed tables and a coded effect size. Each one lists the options, the choice made here, and how much it moves the number.

1. Which group is the control?

Options(a) Small vs. the average of regular and aide, as the authors did. (b) Small vs. regular only.

Choice(b), small vs. regular.

WhyAide classes are the same size as regular classes (22–25). The aide lowers the pupil–adult ratio without shrinking the class, so it’s a different treatment. The coding notes make this point about class size versus PTR. Also, the aide arm was partly reshuffled after kindergarten (p. 560).

EffectSAT reading goes from 0.233 to (521.8 − 505.1)/55.48 = 0.301. Aide classes scored a little above regular classes, so pooling them into the control shrinks the contrast.

2. Which standard deviation?

Options(a) Student-level SD, regular classes (Table 3). (b) SD of class means (backed out of Table 5). (c) Pooled SD across small and regular groups.

Choice(a).

WhyAlmost every other study in a class-size meta-analysis standardizes by variation between students. Class means vary much less than students do (about 17–20 points vs. 42–55), so class-SD effect sizes are 2.5–3 times larger and can’t be mixed with the rest. Option (c) is what Shin and Chung’s formula calls for, but the paper doesn’t print a small-class SD. Using the control SD alone is standard for randomized trials (Glass’s Δ).

EffectSAT reading small vs. others: 0.23 with the student SD, 0.64 with the class SD.

3. Sample sizes the paper doesn’t report

Options(a) Impute from classes × typical size. (b) Split 6,570 equally. (c) Look up the STAR public-use data.

Choice(a), then rescale so the three arms add to 6,570.

WhyThe paper gives classes per arm and typical sizes. 122 × 15, 111 × 22, and 98 × 22 sum to 6,428. Scaling up by 6,570 / 6,428 gives nS ≈ 1,870 and nR ≈ 2,496.

EffectNone on d. Sample sizes only enter the variance, and at these sizes the Hedges correction J = 0.9998. Record “n imputed” in the notes field.

4. Turning pass rates into a d

Options(a) Authors’ route: percentage-point difference ÷ SD of the 0/1 score. (b) Logit route: log odds ratio × √3/π.

Choice(b), metafor’s OR2DL.

WhyThis is the standard conversion for putting a dichotomous outcome on the SMD scale (Borenstein et al., 2009, ch. 7). It also gives the same answer no matter where the pass rates fall on the 0–100 scale. Route (a) depends on how close the pass rate is to 50%.

d= [ ln(.680/.320) − ln(.559/.441) ] × √3π =0.517 × 0.551 = 0.285

EffectBSF reading: 0.249 (a) vs. 0.286 (b). BSF math: 0.162 vs. 0.230. Math moves more because pass rates near 80% compress raw differences. Pick one rule and use it for every dichotomous outcome in the review.

5. Clustering: students in classrooms

Options(a) Use the naive variance that treats all 4,366 students as independent. (b) Inflate it by a design effect.

Choice(b), with the ICC estimated from the paper itself.

WhyTreatment was assigned to classes, and pupils in the same class share a teacher (Hedges, 2007). Table 5 gives just enough to estimate the intraclass correlation. If regular classes have m ≈ 22 pupils, the SD of class means sc and the student SD σ give the between-class variance:

τ2= sc2 − σ2/m1 − 1/m    ICC = τ2/σ2

For SAT reading, sc = 12.9/0.64 = 20.2, so τ2 = (408 − 140)/0.955 = 281 and ICC = 281/3078 = .091. The three SAT scales give .090, .091, and .127, with a mean of .103. (The BSF class-level SDs are on the log-odds scale, so they can’t be used.) With m̄ = 6570/331 = 19.8 pupils per class:

DEFF = 1 + (19.8 − 1)(0.103) = 2.93

EffectThe SE for SAT reading goes from 0.031 to 0.053, and this study’s weight in a fixed-effect model drops by about two thirds. Randomization was blocked within schools, which reduces variance, so treat this as a conservative upper bound.

6. Five outcomes from the same children

Options(a) Enter all five as if independent. (b) Keep all five rows with a shared study_id and model the dependence (multilevel or robust variance estimation). (c) Average them into one reading and one math composite. (d) Pick one outcome per subject.

ChoiceCode all five rows, flag SAT reading and SAT math as primary, and compute composites as a sensitivity check.

WhyCoding everything keeps the later options open. The composite variance needs the correlation between subtests, which isn’t reported. The script assumes r = .7. Option (a) is what makes a pooled SE look too small.

EffectReading composite 0.292 (SE 0.049), math composite 0.273 (SE 0.059).

7. What to leave out

Race subgroups (Tables 4, 6)These are the same students split in two. Adding them alongside the overall rows counts every child twice. Use them only in a moderator analysis that replaces the overall rows. The script computes them for that purpose.

Longitudinal (Tables 9–11)A 35% subset of the same Grade 1 pupils, with no SDs. Leave it out.

SCAMIN (Table 7)Motivation and self-concept aren’t achievement outcomes, and no class-type effect was significant.

Other STAR papersFinn et al. (1989), Achilles (1994), Finn and Achilles (1999), Mosteller (1995), and Nye et al. (1992) all report the same children. The dataset and cohort columns flag the overlap so it can be resolved when the other studies are coded.

Section seven

Comparing with Shin and Chung

What we can check against their paper, and what we can’t.

Shin and Chung’s Table 2 codes this study as: AERJ, 1990, random assignment = 1, data = STAR, subject = R, M, SS, published = 1, grade 1. Their method says each effect size used a pooled SD and the group sizes nS and nL. None of those three numbers is printed in this article, so they must have imputed them or taken them from another source. Their paper doesn’t say which.

  • We can’t recover their per-study effect sizes. They report 120 effect sizes but publish no list. So this replication checks our coding against the primary study, not against their numbers. That limitation is a lesson too: a meta-analysis should publish its coded data.
  • “SS” is ambiguous. Its most likely meaning is SAT word study skills. This study measured no social studies and no science. If SS meant self-concept (SCAMIN), they coded a non-achievement outcome, which their own definition excludes.
  • The magnitude fits. Our SAT effects of 0.29–0.32 (small vs. regular) are above their pooled 0.20, and above their published-study mean of 0.21. That is typical for STAR first grade. The pooled mean includes smaller effects from non-randomized state programs and later grades.
  • This study is one of several STAR reports. Eight of Shin and ChungRoughly ten of Shin and Chung’s 17rsquo;s 17 studies draw on STAR. If each contributes several correlated outcomes, the same children are counted many times, and the pooled SE of .0104 is too small. That dependence problem is the subject of the next replication.

Section eight

Exercises

Each one changes one decision in the R script. Say how much the answer moves.

  1. Switch the control group. Recode all five outcomes as small vs. (regular + aide)/2, using the logit route for BSF. How far do the effects fall?
  2. Use the class SD. Recompute SAT reading with the class-level SD. Suppose a meta-analyst mixed this row with student-SD rows from other studies. Which way would the pooled mean be biased?
  3. Stress-test the imputed n. Set small classes to 13 and regular to 25 pupils. What happens to d? To the variance?
  4. Vary the outcome correlation. Rerun Step 9 with r = .5 and r = .9. When does it matter?
  5. Race as a moderator. Use the Step 10 subgroup effects (small vs. regular) in place of the overall rows. Is the minority–white gap larger on standardized or curriculum-based tests? Compare with the authors’ discussion on pp. 567–568.
