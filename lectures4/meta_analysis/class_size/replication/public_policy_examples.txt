Mertens, S., Herberz, M., Hahnel, U. J. J., & Brosch, T. (2022). The effectiveness of nudging: A meta-analysis of choice architecture interventions across behavioral domains. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 119(1), e2107346118. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118
Synthesizes 212 publications and 447 effect sizes (n ˜ 2.1 million) on choice-architecture interventions, reporting a pooled Cohen's d of 0.45 and moderate publication bias. Intervention (nudges) and outcome (behavior change) are both intuitive. Study-level data and code are deposited on OSF and linked from the article's data-availability statement. Its main pedagogical value is the downstream controversy: two 2022 PNAS letters re-ran the shared data with publication-bias corrections and concluded the adjusted effect is near zero — Szaszi et al., https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200732119, and Maier et al., https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200300119 (Szaszi's reanalysis code: https://osf.io/bntm6/). A ready-made unit on how coding and modeling choices drive meta-analytic conclusions.

Meager, R. (2019). Understanding the average impact of microcredit expansions: A Bayesian hierarchical analysis of seven randomized experiments. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 11(1), 57–91. https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20170299
Aggregates seven RCTs of microcredit access on household profits, consumption, and business outcomes using Bayesian hierarchical models; finds small average effects with modest heterogeneity. The cleanest single-program case on this list. Full data and code are in the AEA replication archive on openICPSR (linked from the article page). Limitation: with only seven studies there is no search-and-screening process to teach; it is better suited to illustrating pooling and shrinkage than sample construction.

Crosta, T., Karlan, D., Ong, F., Rüschenpöhler, J., & Udry, C. (2024, rev. 2025). Unconditional cash transfers: A Bayesian meta-analysis of randomized evaluations in low and middle income countries. NBER Working Paper 32779. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32779
Covers 115 studies of 72 UCT programs in 34 countries across roughly a dozen harmonized outcomes (consumption, income, labor supply, schooling, child anthropometrics), with effects expressed per $100 transferred. Substantively the best match for a policy course: the intervention is transparent and the outcomes are interpretable in dollars. Study-level treatment effects and standard errors are in the online data supplement and have already been reused by other researchers. Caveat: as of the last version I could verify it remains a working paper (also CEPR DP 21216, Feb. 2026), so confirm journal status before citing it as published.

Vivalt, E. (2020). How much can we generalize from impact evaluations? Journal of the European Economic Association, 18(6), 3045–3089. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvaa019
Uses the AidGrade database — about 600 impact evaluations across 20 intervention types (conditional cash transfers, deworming, school meals, microfinance, etc.), double-coded with third-coder reconciliation — to ask how well a meta-analytic mean predicts the next study. Documents the full pipeline from topic selection through search, screening, extraction, and analysis. The coded data are downloadable from AidGrade (https://www.aidgrade.org). Best used to teach the inclusion process and coding protocol rather than a single intervention; the substantive finding (limited generalizability, smaller studies show larger effects) is itself a good discussion prompt.

DellaVigna, S., & Linos, E. (2022). RCTs to scale: Comprehensive evidence from two nudge units. Econometrica, 90(1), 81–116. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709
Compares 126 RCTs run by two U.S. government nudge units (over 23 million participants) with the published academic nudge literature; the outcome is administrative take-up in percentage points. Finds average effects of about 1.4 pp at scale versus 8.7 pp in journals, attributing much of the gap to publication bias and selection of interventions. Econometrica requires a replication package, hosted on Zenodo and linked from the article. A strong public-management example because the "nudge unit" setting and the scale-up question map directly onto practice.

Card, D., Kluve, J., & Weber, A. (2018). What works? A meta analysis of recent active labor market program evaluations. Journal of the European Economic Association, 16(3), 894–931. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvx028
Reviews over 200 evaluations of job training, job-search assistance, subsidized employment, and public-sector employment programs, coding program effects on employment and earnings at short, medium, and long horizons. Substantively ideal — the interventions are familiar and the outcome is employment — and the moderator analysis (program type, participant group, time horizon) is textbook. Data availability is the uncertainty: I could not confirm a posted study-level file; check the JEEA supplementary materials or the authors' sites before adopting it.

Supplementary context

Lakens, D., et al. (2016/2017). Examining the reproducibility of meta-analyses in psychology. https://osf.io/rhccg
Attempted to reproduce 20 published meta-analyses; a third lacked a table of individual effect sizes and five could not be reproduced at all. Useful framing for why the reproduction-file criterion matters.

International Review of Administrative Sciences (2026), 92(1), 22–40. Meta-analysis in public administration research: An interdisciplinary perspective review in the age of open science. https://doi.org/10.1177/00208523251394700 (verify DOI)
Content analysis of 107 PA meta-analyses; the fastest route to checking whether any PA-journal meta-analysis meets your data-sharing bar.
