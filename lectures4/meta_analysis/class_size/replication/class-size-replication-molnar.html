Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Replicating Shin and Chung: Molnar et al. (1999)

This is the third replication case study. Wisconsin’s SAGE program is a quasi-experiment: schools chose to join, and comparison schools were picked afterward. The paper reports raw means, pre–post means, a regression, and a multilevel model. Each supports a different effect size, and they range from 0.17 to 0.33 for the same outcome. This guide works through all four and argues for one. Case studies 1 and 2 come first. The R code is in replication/molnar_1999.R.

Section one

Key concepts

What a quasi-experiment adds to the coding problem, stated as claims.

Key concepts

Without randomization, the choice of estimate is a choice about bias.

In STAR, raw posttest means were an unbiased comparison because assignment was random. In SAGE, schools opted in. The raw posttest difference mixes the program effect with any difference between the groups of schools and pupils. The adjusted estimates try to remove that difference, and each adjustment rests on its own assumptions.

One study, four defensible numbers.

posttest only 0.31  ·  gain scores 0.33  ·  regression-adjusted 0.17  ·  HLM dose–response 0.20

These are all for the 1997–98 total score. Choose the rule before you see which number it gives, and apply the same rule to every quasi-experiment in the review.

The unit of assignment sets the clustering.

SAGE status belongs to a school. There are about 2,100 pupils per cohort but only 44–47 schools, and the standard error has to reflect that.

Check what the treatment actually was.

SAGE lowered the pupil–teacher ratio. It did not always shrink the class: 22% of its first-grade rooms held 30 pupils with two teachers. It also bundled three other interventions. A meta-analysis of “class size” has to decide whether this counts.

Section four

Step one: reproduce the authors’ “0.1 and 0.2”

The text gives effect sizes but doesn’t say which SD they used. Try the candidates.

The 3.3 and 6.3 points are the Table 9 SAGE coefficients for the total score. Two SDs are available. One is the pooled posttest SD from Table 8. For 1997–98 that is 37.5 (from 36.66 and 38.83, weighted by 1,310 and 829 pupils). The other is the residual SD from Table 9 (24.34).

The posttest SD reproduces the text. The residual SD comes from a model that has already removed the variation explained by the pretest, so dividing by it inflates the effect by about half. The authors used the right SD.

Section five

Four routes to d

The same outcome (1997–98 total score) run through each route the paper supports.

1. Posttest only

d= 568.63 − 556.8737.5 =0.313

This is the formula Shin and Chung describe. It ignores the pretest, which is fine only if the groups started equal.

2. Gain scores

d= (568.63 − 519.06) − (556.87 − 519.51)37.5 = 12.2137.5 =0.325

This subtracts each group’s fall mean. But with 37–39% of pupils joining mid-year, the fall and spring means describe partly different children.

3. Regression-adjusted

d= bSAGEspost, pooled = 6.3337.5 =0.169

This compares pupils with the same pretest, lunch status, race, and days absent. The What Works Clearinghouse recommends this rule: the covariate-adjusted difference divided by the unadjusted pooled posttest SD.

4. HLM dose–response

d= −(−0.83) × (22.42 − 13.47)37.5 = 7.4337.5 =0.198

This uses template 4 from the coding notes: slope × size of reduction ÷ SD, with the sign flipped. It uses actual classroom ratios instead of a SAGE label, and it accounts for pupils nested in classrooms.

Why do raw and adjusted differ by half?

The fall means are nearly identical (519.06 vs. 519.51), so adjusting for the pretest can’t explain the gap by itself. The paper points to two other candidates:

  • Different pupils. The regression needs a pretest, a posttest, and every covariate for each pupil. The Table 8 columns each include whoever had that score. With the turnover in Table 2, the regression sample may differ a good deal from the descriptive one.
  • Covariates. In 1997–98, fewer SAGE pupils were eligible for free lunch (43.8% vs. 52.2%, Table 1). Adjusting for SES removes part of the SAGE advantage.

The paper doesn’t report enough to separate the two. Say so in the notes field.

Section six

The decision points

Six choices. The first is the one that matters most for this study.

1. Which route?

OptionsPosttest only, gain scores, regression-adjusted, or HLM dose–response.

ChoiceRegression-adjusted, with the other three stored as sensitivity columns (d_post, d_gain, d_hlm).

WhyThis is a nonrandomized comparison, so the estimate should use the pretest and background controls. The regression does that for the whole sample, reports the numbers needed, and uses the effect-size rule the authors themselves used. The HLM route is attractive, but it is less stable: 1997–98 reading gives 0.06, while the other routes give 0.16–0.26. It also measures a slightly different treatment (continuous ratio vs. SAGE membership).

Effect1997–98 total: 0.17 instead of 0.31.

2. Which SD?

ChoicePooled, unadjusted posttest SD (Table 8).

WhyThis puts the effect on the same scale as the STAR case studies, which used variation between pupils at the posttest. The residual SD from Table 9 would inflate the effect by about 50% (Section four).

3. The variance of an adjusted effect

ProblemTable 9 gives no standard error for the SAGE coefficient. The last row is the residual SD.

ChoiceApproximate the coefficient’s SE from the residual SD, then convert it to the d scale:

SE(b) ≈ sresid √(1/nS + 1/nC)    v = [SE(b) / spost]2

EffectFor the 1997–98 total, v = 0.00083, compared with 0.00198 from the usual two-group formula. The covariates explain much of the outcome variance, and that precision should be counted.

4. Clustering by school

ProblemSchools, not pupils, were in or out of SAGE. There are about 49 pupils per school per cohort, and the paper reports no school-level ICC.

ChoiceAssume a school ICC of .10, a typical value for early-grade achievement after controlling for a pretest (Hedges & Hedberg, 2007), and vary it.

DEFF = 1 + (48.6 − 1)(0.10) = 5.76

EffectThe SE for the 1997–98 total rises from 0.029 to 0.069. With ICC = .05 it would be 0.053, and with .20 it would be 0.093. Of the three case studies, this one depends most on its clustering assumption.

5. Is SAGE a class size study?

ProblemIn 1997–98, 26 of 118 first-grade SAGE rooms (22%) were 30:2, 45:3, or floating-teacher rooms (Table 5). Those are normal-sized classes with extra teachers. SAGE also bundled three other interventions.

ChoiceInclude it, coded measure = "PTR (within classroom)", and run the review with and without such studies.

WhySTAR’s aide classes showed that a second adult in a full-size class does little. Pooling SAGE with STAR assumes a 30:2 room works like two rooms of 15. For the bundled interventions, HLM Model B (Table 12) helps: once the ratio is controlled, the SAGE dummy is not significant, so the ratio seems to carry the effect.

6. Two cohorts, one with a ceiling

Options(a) Use only 1997–98, because the authors say 1996–97 is biased downward. (b) Code both, and flag the ceiling.

Choice(b). The two cohorts are different children, so they are independent samples (though from the same schools). The ceiling column allows a sensitivity analysis.

WhyDropping the cohort with the smaller effect because the authors explain it away is selection on the outcome. A test ceiling is a real measurement problem, but it should be tested, not assumed.

Section eight

Comparing with Shin and Chung

Their Table 2 row for this study, checked against the paper.

FieldShin & Chung, Table 2The paper

Year19951999. Their own reference list says 1999, and SAGE didn’t start until 1996.
Random assignment0Correct
SubjectsR, MReading, language arts, math, total
Grade1, 2Grade 1 only. Grade 2 was tested but not reported.
  • Their formula implies the posttest route. They describe d as a difference in group means over a pooled SD. Applied to Table 8, that gives 0.22 (reading) and 0.33 (math) for 1997–98, about twice the adjusted values.
  • Their definition excludes this study. They say they “used class size as our focal concept rather than the teacher-pupil ratio.” SAGE is a pupil–teacher ratio program.
  • A pattern across the three case studies. Each Table 2 row checked so far has had something to question: an ambiguous “SS” (case 1), duplicate children (case 2), and here a wrong year and grade. None of these errors changes the pooled mean much. Together, though, they show why coded data should be published with the meta-analysis.

Section nine

Exercises

Each changes one decision in molnar_1999.R.

  1. Pick a different route. Use d_post as the effect size. Then pool the primary reading and math rows from all three case studies. How much does the pooled mean move?
  2. The ICC. Rerun Step 6 with a school ICC of .20. Which rows are still significant?
  3. Drop the ceiling cohort. Remove 1996–97 and recompute a SAGE-only mean. Then make the case for and against doing this in a published meta-analysis.
  4. Dose. SAGE cut the ratio by about 9–10 pupils, and STAR cut class size by about 7. Rescale the SAGE adjusted effects to a 7-pupil reduction. Are they now closer to STAR’s 0.30?
  5. Subgroups. Tables 10 and 11 give means for African American and white pupils, but no SDs. Borrow the overall SD from Table 8 and compute gain-score effects for each group. What assumption does borrowing the SD make, and which way would it bias the result?
