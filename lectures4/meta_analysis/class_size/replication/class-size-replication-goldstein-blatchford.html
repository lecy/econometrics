Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

Replicating Shin and Chung: Goldstein and Blatchford (1998)

This is the fourth replication case study. Goldstein and Blatchford wrote a critique of class size research methods and included a short multilevel reanalysis of Project STAR. The effects come already in SD units, reported only for Black and white pupils, and “adjusted” for a pretest taken after a year of treatment. Case studies 1, 2, and 3 come first. The R code is in replication/goldstein_blatchford_1998.R.

Section one

Key concepts

What this case study adds, stated as claims.

Key concepts

When the outcome is a z-score, a regression coefficient is already a d.

Goldstein and Blatchford standardized every score to mean 0 and SD 1 before modeling. The coefficient on “small class” is therefore a standardized mean difference. No conversion is needed, but the standardizer is the SD of all pupils, not the regular-class SD used by Finn and Achilles.

Adjusting for a variable the treatment has already affected changes the question.

In Molnar (case study 3), the pretest came before the treatment, so adjusting for it removed pre-existing differences. Here, the “pretest” is the end-of-kindergarten score. It was taken after a year in small or regular classes, so it already contains part of the effect. Adjusting for it leaves only the extra gain in Grade 1.

pretest before treatment → adjust   |   “pretest” after treatment → don’t, if you want the total effect

Subgroups without an overall effect can be recombined, if you know the weights.

Black and white pupils are different children, so their effects are independent. A weighted average with the right shares gives an overall effect. The variance is the sum of squared weights times each variance.

The same data analyzed by different people gives different numbers.

For the same first graders, Goldstein and Blatchford’s effects differ from Finn and Achilles’ by up to 0.09 SD. A meta-analysis that includes both treats an analytic disagreement as if it were two replications.

Step one: check the tables against themselves

With no means and SDs to recompute from, internal consistency is the check you have.

Do the counts add up?

The column sums (1,442 + 2,806 + 1,624 = 5,872) agree with the cells, and the printed row means are the n-weighted averages of the cells. So the cell counts are right, and two printed math totals have a wrong first digit. This takes one line of R to catch, and only a careful reader would catch it by eye.

Does the text match the table?

By kindergarten class, Table I gives 0.12 − (−0.04) = 0.16 for math and 0.12 − (−0.05) = 0.17 for reading, matching the text. By Grade 1 class, the table gives 0.22 − 0.07 = 0.15 and 0.21 − 0.05 = 0.16, but the text says 0.29 and 0.26. The next paragraph then concludes the difference is “about 0.15” and “the same at the end of kindergarten and the end of grade 1,” which agrees with the table. Code from the table and note the inconsistency.

Cross-check with case study 2. The kindergarten effects of 0.16 and 0.17 are close to Finn and Achilles’ (1999) 0.15 and 0.18. These are the same children, so they should be.

Section five

The decision points

Six choices. The second is the lesson this paper teaches best.

1. Is this a study, or a reanalysis?

Options(a) Code it as an independent study, as Shin and Chung did. (b) Code it as a secondary analysis of children already in the dataset.

Choice(b). Every row is flagged dup_of and left out of the primary analysis. The rows are kept for three uses that the other STAR reports can’t supply: race as a moderator, the adjusted “second-year” effect, and the between-school SD.

2. Adjusted or unadjusted?

OptionsThe “No adjustment” column or the “Adjusted” column of Table II.

ChoiceUnadjusted as the effect of small classes by the end of Grade 1. Adjusted stored as a sensitivity row labeled “second-year effect.”

WhyOf the Grade 1 small-class pupils with a kindergarten score, 84% (1,211 of 1,442, Table I) had been in a small kindergarten class too. Their end-of-K score already reflects a year of the treatment. Controlling for it removes the kindergarten part of the effect. Adjustment usually helps in an RCT, as the authors say, but only for covariates measured before assignment.

EffectAdjustment lowers each effect by 0.05–0.11. White reading drops from 0.13 to 0.04, which is the basis of the authors’ claim that small classes after kindergarten matter mostly for Black pupils. That claim is about incremental gains, not total ones.

3. Which standardizer?

ProblemThe z-scores use the SD of all pupils. Finn and Achilles used the SD of regular-class pupils only.

ChoiceAccept the reported values and record sd_type = "SD of all pupils".

WhyThe all-pupil SD includes some variation caused by the treatment itself, so it is slightly larger. With a 0.2–0.3 SD effect in about 30% of pupils, the difference is under 1%. That is small compared with the other issues here, but it should be recorded rather than assumed away.

4. Subgroups only: recombine or keep separate?

Options(a) Keep the Black and white rows as separate effect sizes. (b) Rebuild an overall effect by weighting them.

ChoiceBoth. The separate rows feed a race moderator analysis. The rebuilt overall is a cross-check against Finn and Achilles.

HowTake the Black share as about one third (Finn & Achilles, 1990, note 6). The groups are separate children, so there is no covariance term:

dall = 0.33 dB + 0.67 dW    vall = 0.332 vB + 0.672 vW

EffectUnadjusted overall: math 0.33, reading 0.19 (SE 0.047 each). Keeping two race rows from the same study is fine here, unlike in case study 1, where race rows and an overall row would have counted every child twice.

5. Variances when nothing is reported

ChoiceImpute from the case study 1 coding: 6,570 Grade 1 pupils, 33% Black, 28.5% in small classes, design effect 2.93.

ResultSE of about 0.082 for Black pupils and 0.057 for white pupils.

CheckThe paper offers an independent check. The effect varies across schools with SD 0.25, and STAR had about 79 schools. The average effect then can’t be known more precisely than 0.25 / √79 = 0.028 from school-to-school variation alone. That is a third to a half of the imputed SEs, so they are the right order of magnitude.

6. Heterogeneity inside one experiment

The findingA between-school SD of 0.25 for reading is “of the same order of magnitude as the overall, adjusted, class size difference.” The effect was negligible or reversed in some schools and large in others.

Why it mattersA random-effects meta-analysis estimates heterogeneity between studies. Here STAR, often treated as one effect size, contains about as much heterogeneity as the whole literature. The authors add a population caveat: STAR only included schools with at least three kindergarten classes, so “any conclusions will apply strictly only to large schools” (p. 257).

CodingRecord the within-study SD in the notes. Code the school-size restriction as a population note for generalization.

Section seven

Same children, different analysts

Grade 1 race-specific effects from two reports on one STAR cohort.

Subject, groupGoldstein & BlatchfordFinn & Achilles (1990)Gap
Math, Black / minority0.400.31+0.09
Math, white0.290.22+0.07
Reading, Black / minority0.320.35−0.03
Reading, white0.130.15−0.02

Neither paper explains the differences. There are four candidates:

  1. Standardizer: SD of all pupils vs. SD of regular-class pupils.
  2. Contrast: small vs. regular and aide pupils pooled by n, vs. small vs. the average of the regular and aide means.
  3. Unit and model: a pupil-level multilevel model vs. an ANOVA on class means.
  4. Sample and measure: “minority” vs. “Black,” and possibly different pupils or reading scales. Goldstein and Blatchford don’t say which reading scale they used.

Back to Shin and Chung. Their Table 2 codes this paper as “Goldstein, et al., 1998, BERJ,” random = 1, STAR, R, M, grades K and 1. Those fields are correct, except that it has two authors, so “et al.” is wrong. It is one of seven entries built on these Grade 1 children. Counting it as an independent study treats a disagreement between analysts as a replication. The 0.09 SD gap in Black pupils’ math is analytic, not sampling, variation.

Section eight

Exercises

Each changes one step in goldstein_blatchford_1998.R.

  1. The weights. Rebuild the overall effect with a Black share of 25% and then 40%. How sensitive is the reading estimate, and why more than math?
  2. Adjusted as primary. Suppose a meta-analyst used the adjusted column for this study and unadjusted effects from Finn and Achilles. What would the combined estimate mean? Write one sentence describing the question it answers.
  3. Attrition. Pupils lost after kindergarten scored about 0.3 SD below average, and more of them came from regular classes (p. 264). Which way would that bias the Grade 1 effect? Is the bias worse for the adjusted or the unadjusted estimate?
  4. Heterogeneity. With a between-school SD of 0.25 and a mean effect of 0.2, what share of schools would you expect to show a negative effect, assuming normality? What does that imply for a district deciding from STAR alone?
  5. A fourth table. Build the moderator dataset: race-specific rows from Goldstein and Blatchford and from Finn and Achilles (1990, Table 6). Fit a model with race as a moderator. Should the two sources be treated as independent? What would you do instead?
