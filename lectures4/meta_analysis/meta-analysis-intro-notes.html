Research Integrity and Evidence Synthesis

What Is Meta-Analysis?

Section one

Key concepts

The ideas the rest of the unit rests on, each stated as a claim.

Key concepts

Meta-analysis is the quantitative synthesis of research results.

A systematic review finds, screens, and appraises every relevant study under a written protocol. A meta-analysis is the statistical step that follows when the studies report enough quantitative information: extract an effect size from each study, then combine them in a model.

A single study is one contribution to a body of evidence, not the final answer.

Individual estimates are noisy. The question “does reducing class size raise achievement?” is answered by the distribution of estimates across studies, not by whichever study happened to be published most recently or most loudly.

Vote counting is not meta-analysis.

  • Vote counting tallies how many studies were significant versus not.
  • It throws away magnitude, ignores precision, and says nothing about heterogeneity.
  • When individual studies are underpowered, it will declare “no effect” even when every study is estimating a real one.

Effect sizes are the common currency.

Studies measure outcomes on different scales — different tests, different units. A standardized effect size such as Cohen’s d expresses each result in standard-deviation units so the results can be compared and combined.

  • A t-statistic answers is there a difference? It grows with sample size.
  • An effect size answers how big is the difference? It does not.

Pooling is a weighted average, and the weights are precision.

Each study is weighted by the inverse of its sampling variance, so large, precise studies count for more than small, noisy ones.

Common-effect and random-effects models make different claims about the world.

  • Common (fixed) effect: every study estimates the same true effect; all spread is sampling error.
  • Random effects: true effects differ across studies; the pooled estimate is the mean of a distribution of effects, and there is an extra between-study variance component, τ2.

Heterogeneity is information, not a nuisance.

Moderator analysis and meta-regression ask why effects differ — by design, by population, by the size of the class-size reduction. In a contested literature, the moderators are often the answer.

A synthesis is only as good as the literature it synthesizes.

If non-significant results go unpublished, the pooled estimate inherits that bias. Funnel plots and trim-and-fill can detect and estimate it; they cannot fully repair it.

Section two

Why synthesize at all?

From narrative reviews and vote counts to formal research synthesis.

The problem meta-analysis was invented to solve

For most of the twentieth century, a literature was summarized by a narrative review: an expert read the studies they could find and wrote an informed opinion. Gurevitch, Koricheva, Nakagawa and Stewart (2018) explain why this breaks down. When there are hundreds of studies on one question, no reviewer can summarize them transparently or reproducibly, and the choice of which studies to read and how to weigh them is invisible to the reader.

Class size is the textbook case. Studies go back to the early 1900s, and by the 1970s reviews kept concluding that the evidence was contradictory. Glass and Smith (1979) described the topic as having dissolved into “cynical despair or epistemological confusion.” Their response — and Glass’s broader insight, with Smith, that outcomes from different experiments could be standardized and put on the same scale — is what launched modern meta-analysis in the social sciences.

Why counting votes gives the wrong answer

The tempting shortcut is to count: twelve studies found a significant effect of small classes, fifteen did not, so the evidence is “mixed.” Gurevitch et al. call vote counting a discredited procedure that nonetheless keeps coming back, and it is worth being precise about why it fails.

  • It confuses power with effect. Whether a study is significant depends on its sample size as much as on the true effect. A literature of small studies can produce mostly non-significant results even when the true effect is the same, and positive, in every study.
  • It gets worse with more data. If each study has less than a 50% chance of detecting the effect, the majority of studies will be non-significant, and adding more studies only makes the wrong majority verdict look more secure.
  • It discards magnitude. A study estimating 0.03 and one estimating 1.06 each cast one vote.
  • It ignores precision. A study of 40 students and a study of 6,000 students count equally.

Reading the forest plot on slide 2

The slide shows a random-effects meta-analysis of 27 studies from chapter 16 of Experimentology. Each row is one study: the square is its standardized mean difference, the whiskers are its 95% confidence interval, and the size of the square reflects the study’s weight. The diamond at the bottom is the pooled estimate: SMD = 0.40, 95% CI [0.20, 0.61].

A vote counter would report that most studies found nothing. The meta-analysis reports a pooled effect of 0.40 standard deviations whose interval sits well clear of zero. Both summaries use the same 27 studies; only one of them uses the information the studies contain.

Two other features of the plot are worth noticing before moving on:

  • The spread is far larger than sampling error. Estimates run from −0.06 to 2.60, and many intervals do not overlap each other. That is the visual signature of heterogeneity, and it is why the slide uses a random-effects model.
  • The biggest effects come from the smallest studies. The heavily weighted studies near the top of the plot sit close to zero, while the largest estimates (2.60 and 1.37) come from studies with small squares and wide intervals. That pattern — a small-study effect — is exactly what a funnel plot is designed to diagnose, and it can be a symptom of publication bias.

Two goals of research synthesis

Gurevitch et al. distinguish two purposes that shape every downstream choice:

Evaluate a specific interventionReach a broad generalization
Typical sizeRelatively few studies (fewer than about 25)Dozens to hundreds of outcomes
Main targetAn accurate overall mean effectSources of heterogeneity
Question framingPICO: population, intervention, comparator, outcomeDeliberately heterogeneous populations and settings
RiskConclusions may apply only to a narrow populationHeterogeneity hard to account for; fishing among moderators

The class size debate sits between the two. The intervention sounds specific, but the studies span tutoring-sized groups and small reductions in large classes, different grades, and designs ranging from cross-sectional correlations to randomized experiments. That is why the moderators — especially study design — end up doing so much work later in the unit.

Section three

Effect sizes: the common currency

What gets extracted from each study before anything is pooled.

A t-score is not an effect size

A t-statistic is a signal-to-noise ratio: the difference between groups divided by its standard error. Because the standard error shrinks with sample size, the same underlying difference produces a larger t in a larger study. That is what makes t useful for testing and useless for comparing studies of different sizes.

An effect size divides the difference by the standard deviation of the outcome instead — a feature of the population, not of the sample size. It tells you how big the difference is in standardized units, which is the quantity a meta-analysis combines.

Cohen’s d

The workhorse standardized mean difference (SMD):

d= M1 − M2SDpooled

where M1 is the treatment mean and M2 the control mean. The general pooled SD weights each group’s variance by its degrees of freedom:

SDpooled= √(n1 − 1)SD12 + (n2 − 1)SD22n1 + n2 − 2

When the groups are the same size this reduces to the simple average of the two variances, √[(SD12 + SD22)/2], which is the version the handout uses.

A worked example: d, g, and Δ

Treatment: M1 = 80, SD1 = 10, n1 = 25. Control: M2 = 70, SD2 = 12, n2 = 25.

  1. Pooled SD. √[(100 + 144)/2] = √122 = 11.05.
  2. Cohen’s d. (80 − 70)/11.05 = 0.905. By Cohen’s conventional benchmarks (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) this is a large effect.
  3. Hedges’ g. d is biased upward in small samples. Hedges’ correction multiplies it by J = 1 − 3/(4N − 9), where N = n1 + n2. Here J = 1 − 3/191 = 0.984 and g = 0.984 × 0.905 = 0.89.
  4. Glass’s Δ. Standardize by the control group’s SD alone, on the logic that treatment may change the spread as well as the mean: Δ = 10/12 = 0.83. It is smaller here because the control group happens to be the more variable one.
g=J × d, J=1 − 34N − 9

With 50 students the correction barely matters (0.905 versus 0.89). With 10 students, J = 1 − 3/31 = 0.90 — a 10% haircut. Many early class size studies were small, which is one reason meta-analysts report g rather than d.

Difference between groups versus change within a group

The same “standardized” language covers two different quantities, and mixing them in one analysis is a classic coding error.

Standardized mean difference (SMD)Standardized mean change (SMC)
DesignBetween subjects: treatment vs. controlWithin subjects: pre vs. post, same people
QuestionHow different are the two groups?How much did the group change?
Formula(M1 − M2) / SDpooled(Mpost − Mpre) / SD, where the SD is the pre-test SD or the SD of change scores
DependenceGroups independentPre and post scores correlated; the correlation r enters the variance
EstimatorsCohen’s d, Hedges’ g, Glass’s ΔMorris & DeShon (2002) variants: dpre, dchange, correlation-adjusted versions

Two quick examples, with the numbers corrected from the source handout:

  • SMD. Treatment mean 80, control mean 70, SDpooled = 10: SMD = (80 − 70)/10 = 1.00.
  • SMC. Pre 70, post 80, SDchange = 15: SMC = (80 − 70)/15 = 0.67.

Correction note. The SMD_vs_SMC.docx handout divides the SMD example by 30 instead of the stated pooled SD of 10 and reports 0.33; the correct value is 1.00. It also rounds 10/15 to 0.66; the correct rounding is 0.67. The conceptual point survives: a 10-point gain is a different effect size depending on whether it is a gap between groups or a change within one group, and on which SD you divide by.

The reason this matters: the SD of change scores shrinks as the pre–post correlation rises, so a change-score effect size can look much larger than a between-group d for the very same raw gain. Put them on a common metric before pooling. The companion page on effect-size conversions works through how.

Section four

Pooling, heterogeneity, and bias

The six panels of Gurevitch et al.’s Figure 1, read as the steps of a meta-analysis.

Step 1: find the studies systematically (panel a)

The PRISMA flow diagram records how many records were identified, screened, assessed in full text, and finally included — first in the qualitative systematic review, then in the quantitative meta-analysis. Its job is transparency: a reader can see how a thousand search hits became thirty studies, and could repeat the process. PRISMA itself is a 27-item reporting checklist plus this template diagram.

Step 2: weight by precision and pool (panel b)

Each study contributes an effect size yi and its sampling variance vi. The pooled estimate is an inverse-variance weighted average:

θ̂= ∑wiyi∑wi , wi= 1vi

This is the same logic as weighted least squares: observations measured more precisely deserve more influence. Gurevitch et al. are pointed about the alternatives. Unweighted averages can be unbiased, but they cannot separate within-study from between-study variation, so neither common- nor random-effects models can be fit, and they give small, noisy studies — which tend to report larger and more variable effects — too much influence.

Common effect versus random effects

Panel b shows two diamonds for the same five studies. They differ because the models answer different questions.

  • Common-effect (fixed-effect) model. Assumes one true effect shared by every study; all dispersion is sampling error. Weights are 1/vi. Its conclusions apply to this set of studies.
  • Random-effects model. Assumes each study has its own true effect, drawn from a distribution with mean μ and variance τ2. Weights become 1/(vi + τ2). Its conclusions generalize to the wider population of possible studies.

Adding τ2 to every weight has two visible consequences: the weights become more equal (large studies dominate less), and the pooled interval gets wider. That is why the purple random-effects diamond in panel b is wider than the brown common-effect one. For a literature like class size — different countries, grades, tests, and size reductions — assuming a single true effect is hard to defend, so random effects is the usual default.

Step 3: quantify and explain heterogeneity (panels c and d)

Gurevitch et al. call quantifying heterogeneity “universally important,” whatever the goal of the synthesis. The standard summaries (Borenstein et al., 2009) are Cochran’s Q, a test of whether dispersion exceeds what sampling error would produce; τ2, the variance of true effects; and I2, the share of observed dispersion that reflects real differences rather than noise.

Explaining the heterogeneity is where the substance lives:

  • Categorical moderators (panel c). Pool separately by subgroup — for class size, randomized experiments versus quasi-experiments versus cross-sectional studies — and compare the subgroup means and intervals.
  • Continuous moderators (panel d). A meta-regression regresses effect sizes on study characteristics, weighting each study by its precision; the bubble sizes show the weights. For class size, the obvious continuous moderator is how many students the reduction removes.

A meta-regression is an ordinary regression whose unit of analysis is the study. Everything you learned about confounding applies: moderators are not randomly assigned, so a design moderator that “explains” heterogeneity may be proxying for grade level, era, or country.

Step 4: check for publication bias (panel e)

A funnel plot puts effect size on the horizontal axis and precision on the vertical axis. Without bias, the points form a symmetric funnel: precise studies cluster tightly around the true effect, imprecise ones scatter widely on both sides. If small studies with small or negative effects never got published, the lower-left of the funnel is empty. Panel e shows exactly that asymmetry; the open circles are studies imputed by trim-and-fill, a sensitivity analysis that estimates what the pooled effect would be if the missing studies were present.

Gurevitch et al. are clear about the limit: the magnitude of publication bias can sometimes be estimated, but it cannot truly be corrected. Meta-analysis can reveal that evidence is deficient; it cannot manufacture the evidence that is missing. That is the bridge back to Unit 23 — pre-registration and registered reports attack the problem at its source.

Step 5: watch the evidence accumulate (panel f)

A cumulative meta-analysis re-pools the evidence each time a study is added in chronological order. The interval narrows as studies accumulate and the estimate settles down. It also shows when the evidence became sufficient to answer the question — and whether early estimates were systematically larger than later ones, a common pattern when first studies are small and later studies are large and better designed.

Section five

What should be clear in my mind?

Check yourself against this list before moving to the class size studies.

  1. A systematic review is the search and appraisal; a meta-analysis is the statistical synthesis of effect sizes that can follow it.
  2. Vote counting fails because significance depends on sample size. A literature of underpowered studies can look “mixed” while every study estimates the same real effect.
  3. A t-score measures evidence against the null; an effect size measures magnitude. Only the second is comparable across studies of different sizes.
  4. I can compute d, g, and Δ from group means and SDs, and explain when each is preferred.
  5. SMD and SMC are different quantities. A between-group difference and a within-group change must be put on a common metric before they are pooled.
  6. Pooling is inverse-variance weighting. Precise studies count more.
  7. Common-effect and random-effects models make different assumptions, and the random-effects interval is wider because it includes between-study variance.
  8. Heterogeneity is explained with moderators and meta-regression, which are observational analyses at the study level.
  9. Funnel plots and trim-and-fill diagnose publication bias; they cannot undo it.

Key takeaways

  • Meta-analysis replaces opinion with a documented, weighted synthesis of every study that meets pre-stated criteria.
  • The forest plot on slide 2 is the whole argument in one picture: most studies are individually inconclusive, and the pooled estimate is not.
  • Effect sizes are only comparable if they measure the same thing — the same contrast, standardized by the same kind of SD.
  • Hedges’ g is d with a small-sample correction; it matters most for small studies.
  • The random-effects mean is the average of a distribution of true effects, not a single universal effect.
  • In a contested literature, the moderators are usually more informative than the grand mean.
  • A biased literature produces a biased synthesis. Research integrity and research synthesis are the same problem viewed from two ends.

Section six

Where this unit goes next

From the logic of synthesis to a real, fifty-year argument.

The rest of Unit 24 applies these tools to one of the most contested questions in education policy: does reducing class size improve student achievement? The case study follows the literature from Glass and Smith’s (1979) pioneering meta-analysis, through Hanushek’s challenge that well-identified studies show small and inconsistent effects, to later syntheses using modern methods.

Everything on this page becomes concrete there. Vote counting versus effect-size synthesis is part of the historical dispute itself. Study design is the key moderator: cross-sectional correlations, fixed-effects and value-added models, and randomized experiments do not give the same answer. And before any of it can be pooled, each study’s estimate — often a regression coefficient on class size rather than a difference in means — has to be converted into a comparable effect size. That translation step is the subject of the companion page, Putting Every Study on One Scale.
